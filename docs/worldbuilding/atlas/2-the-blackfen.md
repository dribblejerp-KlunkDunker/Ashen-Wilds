The drowned country between the Highland Citadels and the sea. Low, wet and grey from the road, and stranger off it than anywhere in Orlen.

## The region

*Open country · about 110 miles east to west and 55 north to south, five days along the Causeway · held by nobody · danger II to V · lore, with new sites marked*

**At a glance.** A drowned basin of reed, mere and peat between the Highland Citadels' scarp to the north and the firm Downs to the south. Before the ash it was a working country of grain, peat, stone and roads. Now its roads are the only safe ground, and they are not safe.

**How it looks.** From the road: grey reeds to the horizon, flat water reflecting a grey sky, dead trees, and here and there a roof. Off the road, and at night, it changes. The underburn glows red under the moss in the east. The meres freeze glass-clear under hoarfrost moons and show the drowned farms beneath. Herons stand in rows in the dead trees. Smoke comes out of the ground.

**The five parts of the fen** (directions and distances follow the Scale and distances tab):

| Part | Where | What it is like | Its places |
|---|---|---|---|
| The north edge | On the upland edge, where the Bell Road comes down | Firmer ground, old farms, the King's road ending | The Wayfarer's Watchhouse and its ground, the foot of the Bell Road, Moss Hythe on the high fen, Bellwether Yard |
| The Causeway country | East from the Watchhouse | The raised road across the worst of the wet, with water on both sides | The Old Causeway, the Fen Gate, the Causeway tower, the Cold Passing, the Leaning Team, Saint Ebbe's at Wick Hollow, the toll house, and Brackenreach at the east end |
| The drowned middle | South and east of the Causeway | Meres, flooded towns, the stake-roads under the water | Low Harrow, the dry ways, Stakeby, the Salt Trace and the White Trees, the Nailhouse, Saint Leof's at Crake Ford |
| The burning east | The far east, past the Causeway's far reach | Peat country, the cutters' banks, and the underburn | The Burning Bank (new), the Drowned Lantern |
| The south and west | Toward the Downs and the sea roads | Firmer, settled, where the towns that still stand are | Dunmere, Merewick, the Nine Stakes, Reedwake, the Corpseway and Saint Orra's Steps, Tavin's road station |

**The four roads.** The Old Causeway east, the Bell Road north to the Citadels, the Salt Trace south toward the sea trade, and the Corpseway west to Saint Orra's Steps. Under the water run the dry ways, which only stake-hands know.

**Weather.** All six of the Smothering's weathers, and the fen has its own names for them: ashfall (*the ordinary*), miasma fog (*the green*, *fen-breath*), sleet, cinder-squall (*the red wind*), hoarfrost, and the Dead Calm. The underburn makes the cinder-squalls.

**What hunts it.** Greymaw on the road verges, harrow eels and bloodwicks in the water, Gallows herons that call everything else to the dying, walking blooms in still hollows, the Blackfen Ogre at its feeding ground, the Unnamed at unnamed graves, ork packs at choke points, and people: the band in grey rags who call themselves Ashbound.

**Old magic.** Staking holds the dry ways and the Causeway's oldest footings. The Keeping's thresholds still hold at Saint Orra's Steps. Little else: the fen was never rich enough to buy the crafts.

## The Wayfarer's Watchhouse and its ground

*Small complex and its surrounding ground · an 8 m stone house in a yard of about 200 paces · held by the refuge of about forty people · danger II to IV · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** The King's house at the fen end of the King's road: a Crown waystation built narrow on purpose, now the last shelter on the north edge of the fen. Everything the fen does, it does within sight of this door.

**History.** Built about S.-140 with the Causeway tower, a courier stable and a road quarry. It *counted* everyone coming off the Causeway, *tolled* carts by the axle and walkers by the pack, *held* road offenders for the assize, and *sheltered* the King's wayfarers. Slates fell under wet ash in S.1. The courier horses were eaten in S.2. In S.3 the Crown warden, Osric Brayne, rode south with the books, and someone cut THE CROWN DID NOT under the lintel. An ogre took the stable from S.4, which ended every argument about widening the door. Refugees moved in in S.12 and began the warning book. This winter goblins came up the drain and the house fled to the Outside Fire.

**How it looks.** A grey stone box 8 m square with 3.2 m walls and a dark plank roof under a skin of ash, its one door facing west. Ash drifts against every wall. From the yard's edge you see, in one look: the house, the ring of dark stone by the west fence, the gallows stump, the Naming Stone, three figures standing in the flooded south-east corner, and the stable piers going off into the reeds to the north-west. Inside it smells of peat smoke, wet wool and tallow. Sound is close and dead.

**Layout.**

1. **The gallows stump and the hard standing.** The King's road ends here in firm ground. Everyone arriving stops here first, because it is where the house can see you.
2. **The Outside Fire.** A ring of dark stone in the lee of the west fence, where the house puts those it will not have inside. Sleeping hollows pressed into the grass, none across another's line to the dark. The doorstep due is set here: one rag, one stick, in a low box.
3. **The yard.** The door lies face-down by the step. By the doorway: the **nail shrine** (A NAIL FOR AN OPEN DOOR. TAKE ONE AND YOU OWE ONE), the chalked **barter board**, and the **ash-post**, a doorpost notched every spring at the deepest drift, thirty notches with one year missing. The yard ditch runs along the south wall, and the drain comes out into it.
4. **The doorway and the Counting Passage.** 1.35 m wide under the lintel, the inside jamb cut *1.35. DO NOT WIDEN.* Then the passage: 1.7 m wide, 3.4 m long, stone both sides scarred at blade height. The clerk's hatch is low in the south wall, bricked up.
5. **The hall.** One room now, the timber partitions burned in a hungry winter. The hearth on a raised plinth against the east wall, with the **drain cover** in front of it. To the north, the **wayfarers' room** (four couriers' bunks once, the goblin nest now). To the south, the **tally room** (the clerk's desk gone, nail holes where the salt bin stood). In the

north-east, the **strong corner**, the warden's chest under the stub of the loft stair, where Joan hides the salt. In the south-east, the **assize corner**, an oak post with the Crown's iron ring, where Edda was tied.

6. **The drain.** A stone culvert a crawl wide, from under the hearth, beneath the passage, out to the yard ditch. It is the goblins' road into the house, and the one shortcut: whoever knows it can get in or out without the door.
7. **The Naming Stone,** on the road out of the yard. Names scratched into its top in many hands.
8. **The Drowned Graves,** the yard's flooded south-east corner, where the Watch buried travellers who died on the Causeway. Squared stones in standing water, most with no names. The Unnamed stand here.
9. **Withy Bottom (the Spore Hollow),** on the west edge: the sheltered hollow with the only clean pool, the water path through it, a lime pit, and the Spore-Choked Cart. Walking blooms come and stand here.
10. **The Long Stable,** to the north-west: the royal courier stable, a double row of stone piers going off into the fog, each cut with a horse's name (new). The Blackfen Ogre's feeding ground and its Gnawed Bone Heap.
11. **The Causeway Delph,** the old road quarry: a flooded cut and spoil heaps, and the ground of a blind quarry-troll. Keep still.
12. **The Roofed Gallery,** in the yard's south-west corner: a short covered way where the gallery-crawler has been seen.
13. **The East Choke Point,** where the yard meets the Causeway. See the next entry.

**Who is here.** About forty people. Margery Reeve (the warning book), Gil Rudd (the door), Joan Tallis (the stores), Ottor Blewitt (the fire), Cress (the rags), Lew Ashby (thatch, and the cough), Winna's two children (new), and the coughers kept in the cold corner or out at the Outside Fire. Goblins in the nest and the drain. The ogre in the Long Stable. The blind quarry-troll in the Delph. The Unnamed in the graves. Walking blooms in Withy Bottom.

**Old magic.** Little. The **ward** Margery learned from Grisel, spoken over salt across a door or a drain. The **naming** at the Drowned Graves. The lintel itself is not a working, though people touch it going out and coming in as if it were.

**Wonder and mystery.**

**The tally room's plaster (new):** the house's children have drawn summer on it in charcoal for thirty years, things none of them has seen, drawn from what the old people told them. A cow with six legs. A sun with a face. A ship with sails. Margery says the first drawing is older than the warning book.

**The bricked hatch** is the one hollow the band never opened. The oldest residents swear Osric Brayne left a sheet in it on his last night in S.3: the families he admitted, and the families he turned away, the same names on both.

**The ash-post's missing year.** Nobody will say who failed to cut it, or why.

**Dangers.** The drain (goblins, II, a swarm III). The ogre (IV) if anyone cooks with the wind toward the Long Stable. The Unnamed (II, III if you do not know the rule). Blooms in Withy Bottom in still air (II). The door, and who decides at it.

**Finds.** The warning book. The muster book. Joan's salt. The doorstep due. Osric Brayne's sheet behind the hatch. Gale's kettle hat on the bone heap. Winna's pot.

**Hooks.** The novel's Part One happens here. In The Long Account the Watchhouse is the north edge of the known world, and the company may pass it on the way to the Bell Road.

## The Old Causeway and the Causeway tower

*A road and its fort · the raised road runs about 60 miles east from the Watchhouse to Brackenreach, five days on foot, with the toll house half way; the tower is three storeys ·* *held by the ork pack until the gap is broken, then by whoever is on it · danger III, IV at the tower · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** The King's road across the worst of the wet: rubble, timber and stone raised a man's height above water on both sides, with nowhere to go if something comes. At its west end, on the first good stone, the tower the Causeway Watch held for twenty-six years without pay.

**History.** The Watch is older than the waystation: it dates from S.-148, and built the tower about S.-140. From S.12, when the Watch left the waystation to the refugees, the tower was its home: unpaid men and women holding the road on an oath to a king who had sailed, taking toll in salt and food. From S.27 an ork pack worked the fen's edge. In autumn S.29 it came over the tower's yard wall under a cinder-squall, killed four of the nine inside, walled the road at the choke point with the tower's own rubble, and denned behind the wall on twenty-six years of tolls.

**How it looks.** A grey bank running dead straight to the horizon between two sheets of flat water, each reflecting the sky so that the road seems to cross nothing. Leaning stone markers at every mile. Black stumps of old fence along the edges. Herons standing in the water on both sides, watching. The tower at the near end is the tallest thing for miles: grey grit, roofless now, its top ragged against the sky.

**Layout (west to east).**

1. **The East Choke Point.** Where the Watchhouse yard meets the road. The fen calls it the Fen Gate now: the pack's wall of tower rubble across the Causeway with one narrow north-east gap a single ork can pass. Behind it, the **plunder pile**: salt and iron uppermost.
2. **The tower yard.** A walled yard on the first good stone. The bell lies on its side in the mud with its tongue gone.
3. **The tower.** One door at the foot, stopped with stone by the pack.

*Ground floor:* the guardroom, the Watch's dead.

*A tight spiral stair* in the north-west corner, turning so that the defender above has the sword arm.

*First floor:* the armoury, and the salt store behind an iron door.

*Second floor:* the muster room with the watch-board and the serjeant's quarters.

*The top:* the roof gone; the bell-loft beam still across the walls. The best view in the fen.

4. **The culvert.** A stone stair from the guardroom floor down inside the wall to a water gate on the fen side, half underwater. The one shortcut: in from the wet, up into the tower, unseen. Rafe and Merrick left this way.
5. **The long straight.** Mile on mile of exposed road with leaning markers, each cut with its number and a wayfarer's horn. **Passing places** every half mile where carts could pull aside, and where Greymaw wait. The first of them east of the Fen Gate is the Cold Passing: Edda's fire for nine winters, the Watch's buried salt, and a carter's cart standing where its oxen were left yoked.
6. **The Leaning Team.** Five horse skeletons tangled in harness under an overturned stone freight wagon. In dry weather people strip its fittings. When the water rises it hides the harness first, so the wagon looks empty.
7. **The toll house.** Halfway: a squat stone house across the road with a toll bar, the Watch's second post. Empty, or denned, depending on the year.
8. **The drowned inns (new).** Three coaching inns once stood along the Causeway. Only their upper floors and signs show above the water now: the *Three Swans*, the *Wayfarer's Rest*, and one whose sign has lost its name.
9. **The east end,** where the Causeway comes down onto firm ground at Brackenreach and the Salt Trace branches south.

**Under the road.** The Causeway stands on four generations of staked footings, driven with the stake-word. Stake-hands say that at its very bottom, under the deepest stakes, the road rests on piers that nobody drove: squared stone, old beyond any record, set too regularly to be natural. Nobody has dug down to see. *(new, rumour only: this touches the Older Work and stays a rumour.)*

**Who is here.** The ork pack and its old grey-jawed leader, until the gap is broken. The Watch's dead in the tower. Carters of the Cinder Factors, when the road is open. Greymaw following the carts. Gallows herons. In Dead Calms this winter, ork packs passing south in silence.

**Old magic.** Staking in the footings: the road should have sunk a hundred years ago and has not.

**Wonder and mystery.** The road seen in a Dead Calm, when both meres are mirrors and anything walking it walks on the sky. The view from the tower top. The bell's missing tongue, which turns up hidden in the bell-loft, wrapped in a Watch cloak (new). Why the pack came: for the salt, or running from something in the deep fen behind it.

**Dangers.** Exposure: you can be seen from anywhere, and cannot leave the road without sinking (V). The pack at the gap (III). Greymaw at the passing places (II, III). Cinder-squalls, which deafen everyone (V).

**Finds.** The Crown mail in the plunder pile (*to whoever has the gap*), Callow's broadaxe and waybill, the tower roster, twenty-six years of salt, the bell's tongue, the watch-board in Pim's hand.

**Hooks.** The novel's chapters 6 to 9 and 20. In The Long Account the Causeway is the way the company leaves the fen.

## The Burning Bank

*Deep place · miles of hollows under the east fen, some chambers big enough to hold a church · held by nobody; the Bank folk (new) live at its edge · danger II to V · (new), built on the lore's underburn*

**At a glance.** Under the peat country of the east fen, the underburn has smouldered since the first winter, eating the peat from below. It has left a world of hollows under a crust of moss and root: ash halls, glowing walls, a buried forest, and things the peat kept for two thousand years.

**History.** The east fen was cutters' country, like Moss Hythe in the north: the fen's fuel was dug here in long black banks. In the first winters households fled and left their cottages burning. The fire went down into the peat, and peat that catches does not go out. For thirty years it has crept along the bed under rain and frost, venting smoke through holes in the moss. Where it has passed, the ground is hollow. The cutters say the bank is *breathing* before a cinder-squall, because the squalls come out of it.

**How it looks.** From above: black cut banks, peat stacks nobody will burn, smoke rising from a hundred holes in the moss, and in places the ground fallen through into a smoking pit (*a fire-well*). Below: grey ash floors knee-deep and soft as flour, roofs of matted root, and walls that glow dull red where the fire is still working, pulsing slowly as air reaches it. It is warm, the only warm place in the fen. It smells of burning and of something older, like tea. Sound dies in the ash.

**Layout.**

1. **The cutters' banks.** The surface: long black peat faces, stacks, the cutters' huts, and smoke from the moss. The way in is a **fire-well**, with cutters' ladders down its side.
2. **The Ash Halls.** The first hollows, burned out long ago and cold enough to walk. Floors of soft ash that hold every footprint. Roofs of root-mat sagging overhead, letting down grey light where the moss is thin.
3. **The Warm Rows.** Where the Bank folk live: rooms cut into a cooled peat wall at the edge of the fire's reach, warm all winter without a stick of fuel. Hangings of reed matting, cooking holes dug down to the glow.
4. **The Black Wood.** A cavern where the fire has burned the peat away from a drowned forest that died before any record: black oak trunks, hard as iron, standing in rows from floor to roof like the pillars of a hall, their roots holding up the ceiling.
5. **The Sleepers.** Where the burn has cut through old bog, the dead the peat kept: people two thousand years old, brown as leather, perfect to the eyelashes, lying in the walls with their hands folded. Some wear ornaments nobody can place. One holds a small bronze bell.
6. **The Red Seam.** The live face: walls glowing red and orange, the heat like an open oven, the floor cracking into fire. Smoke and bad air pool in the low places.
7. **The Steam Hollows.** Where a mere has broken in from above, water meets fire and the chambers fill with roaring steam and hot black pools.
8. **The Trackway.** Deepest of all, uncovered by the burn: a road of split oak planks laid on the old bog, older than the Sleepers, running down and east into the dark further than anyone has followed it.
9. **The held heart: the Bell Pool.** Where the Trackway ends, a black pool in a round chamber. The Bank folk say that on still nights a bell sounds from under the water.
10. **The shortcut.** A fallen fire-well drops from the Steam Hollows straight back into the Warm Rows. Once found, it saves an hour of the climb.

**Who is here.**

**The Bank folk (new),** about thirty people descended from the cutters who would not leave. They cook on the underburn and never need fuel. Most of them cough, because the air down here is bad. The fen fears them and buys their peat-bricks and bog-oak carvings anyway. They are led by **Ada Wellock**, who has lived underground for twenty years and can tell by the colour of a wall how long it has been cooling.

**An ork pack wintering in the Ash Halls (new).** Orks feel the cold badly and will fight anything for a warm, dry roof. Each winter a pack comes in at the north fire-wells and dens in the cooled halls until thaw. The Bank folk keep to the south side.

**Ash-rats** in swarms in the halls, and iron beetles in the cutters' tools.

**Gallows herons** at the fire-wells, waiting for whatever falls in.

**Old magic.** None. It is the one great deep place in Orlen made only by fire. The Sleepers' bronze bell is older than the bell-founders of Hob's Height, and nobody knows whether it was ever a carrying bell.

**Wonder and mystery.** The Black Wood. The Sleepers' faces. The Trackway, and where it was going two thousand years ago, and why anyone would build a road into a bog. The bell under the pool.

**Dangers.** Collapse into the fire, without warning (V). Bad air in low places, which kills sleepers first (V). Steam bursts (V). The wintering pack (III). Ash-rat swarms (I, II in numbers). Fire in the ash itself: a hollow that looks cold can be burning a foot below its floor.

**Finds.** Bog-oak, black and hard enough to make tools. The Sleepers' ornaments, which the Bank folk forbid anyone to take. Peat-bricks for fuel. The bronze bell. Warm shelter for a winter.

**Hooks.** A place the novel can visit if Part Two needs a warm refuge, or the band needs somewhere to hide. In The Long Account, the cinder-squalls that cross the east fen begin here.

## Low Harrow and the dry ways

*A drowned town and the hidden roads under the water · the town is about a mile of roofs; the dry ways run for miles across the drowned middle · held by nobody; Stakeby kept the stakes until it broke up in S.29 · danger II, V in water · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** Low Harrow was a town on land won from the fen, and the fen took it back. Its streets are under the water and its roofs are a road. Under the reeds around it run the dry ways, the drowned older roads that only stake-hands can walk.

**History.** The town was built on reclaimed land behind sluices. When the drainage failed after S.0, the water rose a street at a time. Its people moved up to the upper floors, then the roofs and the embankments, and then mostly left. The dry ways are older than the town: the drowned lines of farm tracks and droves, marked and held with stakes by four generations of the stake-hands of Stakeby, who drove each stake with a word.

**How it looks.** A town seen from above, sunk to its eaves: roof ridges in rows like the backs of animals, chimneys standing out of flat water, a market cross rising alone where the square was. When the water is clear, the streets show underneath, with doors and window-boxes and a handcart still in a lane. Under a hoarfrost moon the water freezes glass-clear and the whole drowned town lies under the ice like a town under a window. The dry ways look like nothing at all: reed, and water, and here and there the grey head of a stake.

**Layout: Low Harrow.**

1. **The embankment.** The approach along the old flood bank, the last dry ground.
2. **The roof road.** Roof ridges, walls and upper windows joined by planks. It goes the long way round, because some roofs will not take a weight.
3. **The stone mouth.** A carved face on a guildhall wall, mouth open, with a rope fixed through it and across a gap of open water. Everyone crossing has to haul hand over hand under that face.
4. **The market cross,** standing out of the drowned square, and the **boat** tied to it. It is always kept bailed and mended. Nobody has ever met its owner, who leaves eel lines baited along the roofs.
5. **The drowned lanes.** The streets below the water, seen when it is clear or frozen.
6. **The chapel tower (new as a site).** Its belfry stands above the water. Its bell hangs below it, under the surface, in the flooded upper chamber. Under ice it seems to sound on still nights. The ice cracking does that.
7. **The sluice-house.** The broken sluice gate and the cellars under it, flooded black: the harrow eels' lair. Bones on the cellar steps.
8. **The last roofs (new).** The camps where the last people lived on the roofs before they left: a cradle lashed to a chimney, a rain-barrel, a door laid flat for a floor, a name chalked on slates.
9. **The drowned end (new).** Past the chapel, a lane, a cart with its ox still in the traces, upright in the water, kept by the cold.

**Layout: the dry ways.**

1. **The stake-heads.** Every stake is notched on its head in a code only stake-hands read: how deep, which way, how old.
2. **The junctions,** where droves crossed. Stakes driven in rings.
3. **The Cold Passing.** Not on the dry ways, but where they come back up onto the Causeway: a passing place east of the Fen Gate, where the Watch buried salt for patrols caught out in a squall. Edda kept the fire here for nine winters, and she showed the band the salt. A carter's cart still stands here, where its oxen were left yoked the night the tower fell.
4. **Stakeby.** The stake-hands' hamlet: houses on piles in a ring of drowned stakes, reached only by the dry ways. It broke up in the autumn of S.29, when the tower fell and the Watch's salt stopped. The houses stand empty, or nearly (new).
5. **The way round the ork wall.** The line Edda drove herself, east past the gap at low water.

**Who is here.** The unseen boat-owner. The stake-hands who were Stakeby, Edda's people, scattered now, who know the word. Harrow eels in every flooded room. Bloodwicks in the warm shallows. Gallows herons on the roof ridges. The band, which uses the dry ways east.

**Old magic. Staking.** The old stakes hold in bog that should have swallowed them a century ago. A stake driven without the word goes down in a season. Only a handful of stake-hands still have the whole word. Edda has half of it, from her father, and does not know which half is missing.

**Wonder and mystery.** The town under the ice. The boat that is always bailed. The bell under the water. The notches on the stake-heads, a language written in wood across the whole drowned middle.

**Dangers.** Rotten roofs and planks (V). Eels that take a wading ankle and roll (II). Cold water that kills in a quarter of an hour (V). A clear moon that shows you to everything. Losing the stake line in fog.

**Finds.** Eels, smoked. Upper-floor salvage. The Watch's salt at the Cold Passing, if the band has left any. The way across the fen that nobody else can use.

**Hooks.** The novel's chapter 6, and Edda's story. In The Long Account, the dry ways are a route only a stake-hand on the roster can lead.

## Saint Orra's Steps

*Great complex and spire · a rock about 120 m high rising out of the west fen, with seven hundred steps to the top and an ossuary inside it · held by the Keepers of the Steps and the Corpseway penitents · danger I above, III below the Lower Door · lore names the place, the Corpseway, the penitents and the warnings; the rest is (new)*

**At a glance.** The only stone that rises out of the fen: a tall dark rock with a pilgrim stair cut round it, a hospice on its shoulder, a lamp-tower on its crown, and inside it the burial galleries where the west fen has brought its dead for six hundred years. Its lamp is still lit every night, and the whole fen steers by it.

**History.** Orra was a beacon-tender of the Keeping who, the story says, kept a fire on the rock through a flood winter so that boats could find it, and named every drowned body that the water brought to her. The Keeping built a chapel, then a hospice, then the stair and the tower. The rock was hollowed for centuries into an ossuary, every body brought up the Corpseway named and laid in a niche with its name cut above it. In the first winters of the Smothering the dead came faster than anyone could name them. In S.2 the Keepers carried the unnamed dead through a door at the foot of the galleries and walled it, and cut on the bench outside: *avoid a lower door*.

**How it looks.** From miles off: a black tooth on the western horizon with a single point of yellow light at its top every night. Close: dark stone streaked with ash, the stair winding round it in switchbacks, shrines in niches at every turn, the hospice's roofs stepped up the south shoulder, and the lamp-tower thin and tall on the crown. Inside the rock: cold, dry, perfectly quiet, galleries of niches going back into the dark, and names cut on every surface, small and close as writing on a page.

**Layout.**

1. **The Corpseway's end.** The last paired resting benches, where stretchers are set down off the mud, cut with names and warnings. The last bench reads *avoid a lower door*.
2. **The Foot Gate and the almonry.** A gatehouse in the rock with a hatch where the dead are received and named, and a room where the penitents sleep between journeys.
3. **The corpse-lift.** A rope lift up the sheer east face from the Foot Gate to the hospice, for stretchers. It has been broken since S.9. Mending it opens the shortcut from the hospice back to the foot.
4. **The Pilgrim Stair.** Seven hundred steps in eleven switchbacks round the rock, a shrine at every turn. At the third turn, behind a wall, a spring: *water beyond a wall*, as the benches say.
5. **The hospice.** On the south shoulder: three wards stepped up the rock, an infirmary, a herb garth on terraces in the lee where things still grow, and a kitchen whose hearth has not gone out since S.0.
6. **The Chapel of Saint Orra,** cut into the rock: a long low room with her beacon-fire carved over the altar, and the first names on the walls, worn almost smooth.
7. **The Candle.** The lamp-tower on the crown: a stair up the inside to a lamp-room with glass on four sides, kept lit every night with oil the fen sends as offering. The widest view west of the Causeway tower.
8. **The Upper Ossuary.** Galleries running back into the rock, niches floor to ceiling, every niche with its name cut above it. The named dead lie still. The air is dry and nothing fruits.
9. **The Register Hall.** Where the names are kept twice: cut in stone in the galleries, and written in the Steps' register, which is the only complete record of the west fen's dead. People come from as far as Dunmere to find their families in it.
10. **The Lower Door.** At the foot of the deepest gallery: a walled doorway with the Keeping's threshold-words cut round the frame, and a cold coming through the mortar.
11. **The held heart: the Lower Ossuary.** The dead of S.1 and S.2, laid in rows without names because there was no time. Thousands. The threshold holds them in. Whatever stands up behind that wall stays behind it, for now.

**Who is here.**

**The Keepers of the Steps (new):** five old women and men who stayed when the priests went south. They elected themselves, as many parishes did. **Mother Hilde Brennock** leads them and has named more dead than anyone alive. **Brother Bede**,

blind, keeps the Register Hall by touch, reading the cut names with his fingers.

**The Corpseway penitents,** who believe the survivors were left behind to pay a debt. They carry the sick and the dead to the Steps and keep the warnings on the benches. **Avice Stoke** is one of them.

**The sick in the wards,** about twenty at any time, many with the cough.

**Below the Lower Door,** the Unnamed in their thousands.

**Old magic. Keeping.** The namings in the Upper Ossuary still hold: nothing stands up there, and nothing fruits. The **threshold-words** cut round the Lower Door are an old warding that still holds the unnamed dead in. The Keepers renew them each spring with salt and a speaking. *Staunch* is still practised in the infirmary.

**Wonder and mystery.** The Candle seen across the fen at night. The name-walls, millions of names, the whole history of the west fen written on stone. Brother Bede reading the dead with his fingertips. Whether the threshold will hold if nobody renews it.

**Dangers.** Above the Lower Door, very little: the stair in ice (V), and the cough in the wards. Below it: the Unnamed in numbers no naming could reach (III, IV), in the dark, in a maze of galleries.

**Finds.** The Steps' register. Clean water at the third turn. Herbs from the garth. *Staunch*, if a Keeper will teach it. A family's name.

**Hooks.** The novel can send Rafe here if he needs a namer, or Merrick needs a healer. In The Long Account, the Candle is how the company finds the west fen at night, and the register is where Gytha's receipts meet the names of the people they were owed to.

## Dunmere

*City · a walled town on a mound in a mere, about a quarter mile across inside the walls, with lanes under the mound · held by the Wardmote, about three hundred people · danger I inside the inner wall, II and III in the outer town and the Pit Ward · the lore has Dunmere as a walled town with a gate, an inn, a livestock yard and a refuge gate in S.1; the rest is (new)*

**At a glance.** The fen's last town with a wall: a market town built round an old hill-fort on an island of firm ground in its own mere, at the fen's southern edge. In S.1 it opened its gates to the whole south fen and could not feed it. What survives inside the inner wall survives on law.

**History.** The *dun* was an old fort on the only high ground in the south fen, and the town grew round it on market days for a thousand years: ribbons, ironwork, hot food, a livestock yard. In the first autumn of the Smothering the first refugees came from Alder Fold asking for one night inside the walls, and were given the covered side of the inn yard. By the winter of S.1 there were four thousand people inside walls built for six hundred. The Wardmote set the **Conditions**: residence, a useful trade, provisions brought, current registration. Families who failed them were put outside, into the camp that grew in the lee of the wall. In S.2 came fever, the cough and a riot at the granary. The town calls it **the Collapse**. Three in four of the people inside died or left, and the camp outside died almost to the last.

**How it looks.** From the Salt Trace: a dark mound rising out of grey water with a ring of wall round it, a second ring higher up, and over everything the needle spire of the town church. A single causeway runs out to the gate. Inside: steep lanes of grey stone and slate stepping up the mound, half the houses roofless, the upper town lived in, smoke from perhaps forty chimneys. At night one lantern burns in the gate arch.

**Layout.**

1. **The Lee.** The ruins of the camp outside the wall where the refused lived: shelters of hurdle and sod against the stone, and the dead of S.2 where they lay. Corpse-caps grow here every autumn.
2. **The Mere Causeway and the gate.** One narrow causeway to a gatehouse with two towers. The **Conditions** are cut into the arch, and the gate-reeve reads them aloud to everyone who asks to come in.
3. **Garran's Lantern.** A lamp kept burning in the gate arch every night since the first autumn, *for the ones not yet come*. It is named after a man who stood at this gate with a borrowed lantern looking for his son.
4. **The outer town.** The lower lanes inside the first wall: roofless, empty, picked clean, the livestock yard grown over. Ash-rats, Greymaw that come over the wall in hard frosts, and scavengers. The town no longer holds it.
5. **The Pit Ward.** A walled quarter of the outer town where the Collapse dead were put when the lime ran out. It is shut, and walking blooms stand in its lanes.
6. **The inner wall** and its one gate, kept by the Wardmote's watch.
7. **The market square,** still held every week, with the old inn on its north side. The inn yard's covered side is still where newcomers sleep their one night.
8. **The Moot Hall,** where the Wardmote sits and the town's registers are kept. Three of them have been burned and rewritten since S.1, and everybody knows it.
9. **The church of Saint Wenna the Ferrywoman,** at the top of the mound, and its **spire**, the tallest thing in the south fen. Its bell was cast at Hob's Height and has not been rung since S.2.
10. **The Undercroft.** Under the mound, the old fort's tunnels and the medieval granary vaults cut into it: miles of low passages, cisterns and store-rooms. The riot of S.2 happened here. Parts of it have been walled off since, and nobody alive has walked all of it.
11. **The held heart: the Granary Vault,** the deepest store under the church, where the town's seed grain is kept under lock, and where the Wardmote decides each winter who eats.

**Who is here.**

**The Wardmote (new),** twelve householders who govern by the Conditions, which they have never repealed. **Reeve Aldous Penk** chairs it and believes, with reason, that the Conditions saved the town.

**Ysolde Vey (new),** the gate-reeve, who reads the Conditions at the gate and keeps Garran's Lantern lit. She is the Vey family's granddaughter, and she has turned away more people than anyone alive.

**The Moot clerks,** who keep the registers, and the families who want them burned again.

**The Lee's ghosts:** nobody lives there, but the fen says the refused still come to the wall in fog.

**Beasts:** ash-rats in the granary, Greymaw in the outer town, walking blooms in the Pit Ward.

**Old magic. Keeping:** the threshold of Saint Wenna's church still holds. **Carrying:** the spire's bell is a Hob's Height bell. The Wardmote argues every winter about whether to ring it for help, and every winter decides the risk is too great.

**Wonder and mystery.** The spire seen across ten miles of water from the mill road at Merewick. Garran's Lantern. The Undercroft's walled-off passages. Whether Oren Vey ever came.

**Dangers.** The law: a stranger who stays past one night without meeting the Conditions is put out (the town's own danger). The Pit Ward (II, III). The Undercroft's dark (V). Greymaw in the outer town in frost (II, III).

**Finds.** Seed grain, if you are worth it. The town's registers, which hold the south fen's debts and claims. Ironwork from the last smithy. A night's shelter, once.

**Hooks.** A city the novel can mention as the place the Watchhouse would become without its door rule. In The Long Account, Dunmere is where the south fen's receipts are kept, and where Gytha's ledger meets a town that already keeps one.

## Brackenreach

*Town · a broken market town across a river at the Causeway's east end, about half a mile from end to end · held in part by the Upper Row, about ninety people · danger II, V in the Lower Market · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** A freight town where an upland stream became navigable to shallow boats: warehouses, a livestock square, a tannery quarter and a broad bridge. A failed weir drowned its lower streets, and its own people stripped the bridge for barricades. It is the Watchhouse's nearest market, and it sells what it does not own.

**History.** Brackenreach existed because the road met the river. In good years it moved grain down to the boats, hides up from the tanneries and timber everywhere. After S.0 the weir failed and the river spread into the Lower Market. In S.4 the Upper Row barricaded its shopfronts and pulled up the bridge's planking to do it, cutting off the Far Bank. Since then the town has lived by selling salvage access to its own ruins.

**How it looks.** A long ridge of three-storey timber-and-brick buildings above a grey spread of water with roofless warehouses standing in it. The bridge's stone piers march across the river with nothing on them but a single rope. On the Far Bank, workshops with their doors open on the water. From the Causeway you see the warehouse gables first, black against the sky, and the tannery chimneys.

**Layout.**

1. **The Causeway's end,** where the road comes down onto the river terrace. The old toll post, empty.
2. **The Upper Row.** A single street of shopfronts barricaded with doors, carts and bridge planking. Ladders up to first-floor entrances, pulled inside at night. About ninety people live behind it.
3. **The livestock square,** at the Row's west end: empty pens, a well with a lock on it, and the market cross where salvage access is bought and sold.
4. **The Lower Market.** The drowned streets below the Row: roofless stores and flooded cellars, reached by planks and boats. Salvage, eels, and floors that give way.
5. **The tannery quarter.** Pits that still stink after thirty years, a drying loft, and lime enough to cure a hundred hides, which the town keeps secret.
6. **The wheelwright's yard (lore).** Seasoned wheelwright timber under a failing roof, the best in the fen. The roof will come down on whoever takes it.
7. **The bridge.** Five stone piers and a rope. Crossing it means hand over hand above fast water.
8. **The Far Bank.** Cut-off workshops: a smithy, a cooperage, a ropewalk with its long shed intact. Nobody lives there, and Greymaw den in the ropewalk.
9. **The weir (new as a site).** The broken weir upstream, where the river comes down in a white roar. Fixing it would drain the Lower Market in a season, and the Upper Row would lose its salvage trade.

**Who is here.** The Upper Row households, who sell salvage access and do not guarantee that you are the only one who bought it. **Piers Ferrant** (lore) sells ladder passage and salvage rights. Cinder Factors' carters on the Causeway run. Greymaw on the Far Bank. Harrow eels in the Lower Market.

**Old magic.** None. Brackenreach was a town of trade, and trade did not buy the crafts.

**Wonder and mystery.** The ropewalk on the Far Bank: a shed four hundred paces long and perfectly straight, where rope was walked out in one piece, still hung with a half-finished cable that runs its whole length. Who keeps the market cross swept.

**Dangers.** Rotten floors and drowned cellars (V). The bridge rope (V). Greymaw on the Far Bank (II, III). Selling and buying: payment does not guarantee possession.

**Finds.** Wheelwright timber. Tannery lime. The ropewalk's cable, which is worth more than anything in the town. Tools and cart parts.

**Hooks.** The Watchhouse's market for tools and salvage. In The Long Account, the company's first market after the fen.

## Merewick

*Village · a mill village of about sixty people on a tributary at the fen's rising edge · held by its own households, with Mara Venn keeping the shared records · danger I, II at the water ·* *lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** A working village that lived because its mill still turned. When its priest fled, its people cut the parish register apart with sewing shears so that every family could keep its own rights, and they have been arguing about the pieces ever since.

**History.** Merewick sits where a shallow stream drops off the higher ground into the fen, with enough fall to turn a wheel. Its miller held the diversion grant, with duties: keep the bridge, let the water pass downstream. On the ninth day without a priest, the villagers ate the chapel hens. On the tenth, they found his empty travelling chest. Mara Venn, who had cleaned the chapel for nineteen years, carried the register to the altar, and the village cut it up there: three readers to each leaf, births into a flour sack, burials into the gravedigger's chest. Years later a collector came with certified southern copies, and the village learned which of its scraps would stand.

**How it looks.** A cluster of stone houses up a slope, a squat chapel with a record room built on its side, a mill with its wheel still turning in a race of brown water, a patched bridge, and below it the reed plots in neat strips. The mill's shutter has a pale shaved patch where a mark was cut and removed.

**Layout.**

1. **The patched bridge,** kept by the miller's duty. Its supports shift every winter.
2. **The millpond and the diversion channel,** where upstream runoff and debris start every quarrel.
3. **The mill,** wheel turning, the miller's measures on an open table so anyone can watch them levelled.
4. **The chapel and the record room.** Mara's stitched books between boards, the copies with their presenters and witnesses, the shared fragments in chests with three keys.
5. **The higher houses,** where the households keep their own scraps sewn into gloves, crocks and curtain cloth.
6. **The reed plots** downstream, each boundary run from a missing post to a bent willow, and Eda's grant among them.
7. **The gravedigger's shed,** where burial scraps are grouped by place and time in wrappers.

**Who is here. Mara Venn**, the cleaner who became the village's copyist and was accused of appointing herself priest. **The miller** and his son, and the hooked mark that means *interested witness*. **Eda**, the orphan who keeps her reed grant in two copies. **Harl's widow**, whose cart claim is deferred, not discharged. Sixty people who scrutinise every written line.

**Old magic.** None, though the village half-believes the register itself is a kind of working: a name written there holds.

**Wonder and mystery.** The record room, where a whole village's lives are kept on stitched-together scraps, every one read aloud three times. The missing continuations, which could still discharge a debt or prove a marriage if anyone found them.

**Dangers.** Very few from beasts. The danger is the writing: a claim a collector can collect.

**Finds.** Flour. Reeds. The Merewick copies, which can prove or undo claims across the fen. The register's record of an unnamed woman buried beside a sluice with red thread at her cuff, and a smaller child.

**Hooks.** The red-thread woman is the same mystery as Cress's mother in the novel. In The Long Account, Merewick shows what happens when a ledger meets the people it lists.

## The Drowned Lantern

*Small complex · a coaching inn on the Causeway's far reach, three storeys and a lantern tower · held by the band who call themselves Ashbound, about a dozen people · danger III ·* *lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** The last coaching inn on the far reach of the Causeway, roofless above and dry below, where the band in grey rags winters and keeps its tally of every share it has ever given out.

**History.** The inn stood at the far reach's one firm island, where coaches changed horses before the long straight. It had a lantern tower so that coaches could find it at night: the light that gave it its name. After S.0 its keepers left. The roof fell in S.9. Corwen Dole, the Causeway tower's old quartermaster, brought the band here after he was turned away from the Watchhouse door in S.19 and buried his wife in sight of it.

**How it looks.** A big stone inn on a low island with water lapping its front steps. The upper floors stand open to the sky. The lantern tower at its corner still has its glass, and in fog the band lights it, so that it shines across the whole east fen like a ship's light.

**Layout.**

1. **The landing,** where the Causeway's old spur comes onto the island, and a boat kept by the band.
2. **The yard and the stables,** roofless, used for the band's hand-carts.
3. **The taproom,** the only roofed room above ground, with the **tally wall** behind the bar: thousands of notches in rows, every share the band has given.
4. **The upper floors,** open to the sky, where lookouts sit in the rain.
5. **The lantern tower,** a spiral stair to the lamp-room and its glass.
6. **The dry cellars,** stone-vaulted, where the band sleeps and keeps its stores: the Watch's salt from the Cold Passing, the Watchhouse's rope, the healer's needles.
7. **The brew-cellar,** below the dry cellars, flooded waist-deep and black, with eels.

**Who is here. Corwen Dole, the Doleman**, who measures by tally and has never cheated a share. **Osgar**, his second, who would sell anything. **Cuddy**, who ties the knots and coughs. **Wynn Carrow**, a Watch archer who left four years before the tower fell. **Col Wray**, seventeen, Edda's nephew. A handful more.

**Old magic.** None.

**Wonder and mystery.** The lantern in fog. The tally wall. Whether Dole has ever given less than a share to anyone, and whether it matters, given how the shares were got.

**Dangers.** The band (III). The brew-cellar's eels (II). The far reach's exposure.

**Finds.** The Watch's salt, what is left of it. The Watchhouse's rope and needles. The tally wall, which is a record of everyone the band has fed.

**Hooks.** The novel's chapter 18. In The Long Account, the band may still be here, or may have become something else.

## The Salt Trace and the White Trees

*A route and its meeting place · the Trace runs about 36 miles south-west across the fen, four days with its crossings, then on through the Downs to Wexhaven, ten days more · held by nobody; the Cinder Factors know its crossings · danger II, V at the crossings · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** Not one road but many: embankments, towpaths, shallow crossings and boat transfers toward the southern trade, different for every load and every season. Halfway along it, the White Trees: pale dead trunks full of herons, a ruined salt store, and a landing where everyone meets.

**History.** Salt came up from the coast on this line for centuries, by pack and by boat. Its name remembers a cargo lost from a wet pack. After S.0 its crossings shifted every year, and only the **Cinder Factors**, the fen's carrying company, keep track of them: which bank holds, which ford has moved, which towpath is underwater. The White Trees were a stand of willows by the salt store, killed by the first floods and bleached by thirty winters.

**How it looks.** Low banks and towpaths threading flat water, marked with posts, rags and cairns that change meaning by the season. The White Trees stand up out of the fen like bones: a grove of pale trunks with no bark, and in every crook a grey heron, all of them

facing one way. Under them, the salt store's fallen walls and a stone landing.

**Layout.**

1. **The Trace's mouth,** at the Causeway's east end by Brackenreach.
2. **The embankments,** firm in summer, sinking in winter.
3. **The towpath,** where boats were hauled by horses, now walked.
4. **The three crossings (new names):** *Long Wade*, a ford that has moved twice in ten years; *the Ferry Stump*, where a rope ferry ran and a raft is still sometimes left; and *Pole Bridge*, a bridge of lashed poles the Cinder Factors rebuild every spring.
5. **The White Trees.** The grove, the heron rookery, the fallen salt store, the transfer landing. Fresh steps cut in one trunk make a lookout over both approaches.
6. **The southern reach,** where the Trace leaves the fen for the Downs and the sea roads.

**Who is here.** The **Cinder Factors'** carriers and their waybills (*paid on arrival, never before*). Travellers meeting at the White Trees. Gallows herons by the hundred. Greymaw following loads. Bloodwicks at the crossings. In the novel, Pim Haddow, dying in the lookout he cut.

**Old magic.** A few of the oldest marker posts on the Trace are staked with the word. They are the only ones that never move.

**Wonder and mystery.** The rookery facing one way. A heron cry that carries across the whole fen.

**Dangers.** Crossings that have moved (V). Greymaw on the loads (II, III). The herons, which call everything to anything that bleeds.

**Finds.** Salt, oil, needles and news from the south. The salt store's last sacks, spoiled. The Factors' route-knowledge, which is worth more than their cargo.

**Hooks.** The novel's chapter 21. In The Long Account, the Trace is the way south to Wexhaven.

## The Corpseway

*Road · 23 miles west from the Watchhouse to Saint Orra's Steps, two days with a stretcher · kept by the Corpseway penitents · danger II · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** The old parish road of the dead: a raised track across the west fen with paired low stone benches every mile, so that a stretcher never has to touch the mud, and on every bench the names and warnings of the people who carried their dead this way.

**History.** For six hundred years the west fen carried its dead to Saint Orra's Steps along this road, and the parishes kept the benches. After S.0 the parishes failed and the dead did not stop. The **Corpseway penitents**, who believe the survivors were left behind to pay a debt, took the road over: they carry the sick and the dead to the Steps for anyone who asks, and cut practical warnings on the benches as they go. Carrying the dead is the only work in the fen that has never once gone unpaid; nobody pays.

**How it looks.** A grey line of embankment across reed and water, and every mile two low stone benches facing each other across the track, worn hollow on top by a thousand stretchers. Names cut everywhere, small and close, and among them lines like *water beyond the wall*, *avoid the lower door*, *the ford has moved*. On a clear night the lamp of the Steps is ahead the whole way.

**Layout.**

1. **The parish end,** at the Watchhouse's west edge, where a stone cross marks the start.
2. **The bench line,** twenty-two pairs, one a mile.
3. **The Weeping Bench** (new), at the eleventh mile, where the penitents cut the names of the people they could not carry.
4. **The halfway shelter** (new), a turf-roofed bier-house at the twelfth mile where carriers sleep with their dead.
5. **The last mile,** on a raised bank across open water.
6. **The Foot Gate** of the Steps.

**Who is here.** The penitents, among them **Avice Stoke**. Travellers with handcarts. People waiting at a bench for a companion who went ahead and did not come back. Greymaw that follow stretchers. Gallows herons on the bier-house roof.

**Old magic.** Keeping, in the plainest form: the dead are named at every bench they rest on. Whether that does anything is the fen's oldest argument.

**Wonder and mystery.** The bench names: the whole of the west fen's grief in small letters, a road you can read for two days. The Weeping Bench, longest of all.

**Dangers.** Greymaw (II, III as a pack). The Unnamed, where a carried body was left on a bench unnamed (III). Fog on the open-water mile.

**Finds.** Whatever the dead carried and the carriers could not: a broken handcart, an account left beside a body, a Clean Rag, a lamp. Bier-poles in the halfway shelter. Lime, sometimes, left on a bench for the next carrier.

**Hooks.** Carry someone to the Steps. Read the Weeping Bench for a name you know. Finish a carry that a dead carrier began.

## Saint Ebbe's at Wick Hollow

*Chapel · two days east of the Watchhouse: 19 miles of Causeway and 6 of track north · empty since the autumn of S.29 · danger II · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** A small chapel in a hollow off the Causeway, kept open by its cleaner alone for seven years after the priest left, with the Watch's fair copies of every winter's admittance roll still in its vestry press: the proof of who revised the Watchhouse's record.

**History.** Swept by its cleaner from S.10 to the autumn of S.29, under Father Anselm Crewe, who stayed when his superiors left and then left too, one night in S.22, with the silver lamp. The cleaner kept it open seven more years. The Watch lodged a fair copy of each winter's admittance roll here for safe keeping, in the hand of whoever kept the Watchhouse door that year. When the tower fell the cleaner left, and the chapel has been empty since.

**How it looks.** A grey chapel in a bowl of ground, sheltered from the wind, with a targe hung over the door as an offering to the soldier-saints who held gateways. Inside, swept floors under a season's dust, an altar with no cloth, and the vestry door shut.

**Layout.**

1. **The track** north off the Causeway at its nineteenth mile.
2. **The hollow and the chapel yard,** where the cleaner's son **Wilkin** is buried under a wooden horse.
3. **The nave,** its altar cloth long since boiled for rags.
4. **The vestry and its press,** locked; the cleaner knows where the key is.
5. **The oil store,** with three jars left.
6. **The bell-cote,** empty: the bell was taken down and buried in the yard in S.1 (new), so it would not be rung.

**Who is here.** Nobody now. Greymaw in the hollow's rim in hard winters.

**Old magic.** Keeping: the threshold was kept by a cleaner, not a priest, for seven years, and nobody can say whether that counts.

**Wonder and mystery.** A chapel swept clean for seven years with nobody to see it. The buried bell.

**Dangers.** Greymaw (II). The track in fog.

**Finds.** The fair copies in the vestry press. Lamp Oil in the oil store. A targe over the door. The buried bell, if anyone digs. Chapel pitch and tallow torches, dipped like lamp-wicks.

**Hooks.** The fair copies: who changed the Watchhouse's record, and what telling Gil Rudd will cost. Replace Wilkin's horse.

## Saint Leof's at Crake Ford

*Chapel and village · about 40 miles south of the Watchhouse, at a ford in the south fen · Crake Ford's households, about forty people · danger I · lore, with new details marked*

**At a glance.** A ford chapel where, in the first winter, a curate opened the doors to everyone on the road, and half of those inside died: fifty-one names. The village barred the curate, nailed the chapel shut, and has kept its own doors shut ever since.

**History.** Its priest sailed in S.1. In the worst of that winter the curate opened the chapel to everyone on the road, as the Keeping said they must. A fever and a grey cough went through the crowd, and half of those inside died before the thaw; the houses that kept their doors shut mostly lived. Crake Ford barred the curate, who swore against the ford-stone never again to add to the dead by their own hand, and went on the roads as a hedge-priest. Ottor Blewitt was ten and inside; his mother and two sisters are among the fifty-one.

**How it looks.** A grey stone chapel at the water's edge, its doors nailed shut from the outside since S.2 (new), a graveyard of fifty-one small stones all named, and a village of shut doors on the rise behind it. The ford-stone stands in the shallows, a flat-topped boulder worn by hands.

**Layout.**

1. **The ford and its stone,** where the oath was sworn.
2. **The chapel,** nailed shut; its windows boarded; its door scored where someone tried to open it once.
3. **The chapel yard,** fifty-one graves.
4. **The village of Crake Ford,** about forty people in a dozen houses that do not open to strangers.
5. **The ferry path** (new), a stake-marked line across the shallows to the Salt Trace's Downs road.

**Who is here.** Crake Ford's households. Every year on the longest night, someone who comes to the ford-stone and says fifty-one names in order.

**Old magic.** Keeping. Saint Leof's threshold was spoken in the old way, and the fifty-one died inside it anyway: the fen's favourite argument about whether the old words do anything.

**Wonder and mystery.** The longest night at the ford: one voice in the dark saying fifty-one names, and the whole village listening behind its doors.

**Dangers.** The village (I, if you knock). The ford in spate.

**Finds.** Inside the nailed chapel, untouched since S.2: the dead's belongings, piled in the nave by the survivors who would not touch them; a curate's book of the names, in order; blankets and a cask of altar wine. Nobody in Crake Ford will open it. Opening it is a choice.

**Hooks.** The hedge-priest's door, and Ottor. Open the chapel, or don't.

## Small sites

Shorter places of the fen. The four lost towns that were once only names in the archive now have full entries below.

### Stakeby

*Hamlet on piles · in the drowned middle, about 31 miles south-east of the Watchhouse, by the dry ways only · broken up in the autumn of S.29 · danger II · lore name and its breaking; layout and details (new)*

**At a glance.** The stake-hands' hamlet: six houses on oak piles in a ring of drowned stakes, reached only by the dry ways, where four generations kept the knowledge that holds the Causeway's footings and marks the roads under the water.

**History.** The families of Stakeby were paid by the Crown to drive and keep the stakes, and after S.3 by the Watch's salt. They knew the stake-word. When the tower fell in the autumn of S.29 the salt stopped and Stakeby broke up. Edda set out with three neighbours, and two of them reached the Outside Fire.

**How it looks.** A ring of black stake-heads standing out of flat water, and inside it six grey houses on piles, joined by plank walks, with a tall timber tripod in the middle where the young learned to drive. No smoke, unless someone stayed.

**Layout.**

1. **The count,** the outer ring of drowned stakes, which the stake-hands call a fence.
2. **The landing,** where the dry ways come up.
3. **The six houses** on piles, joined by plank walks.
4. **The driving frame,** a timber tripod with a beetle on a rope.
5. **The stake-yards** under each house, where seasoned oak is kept wet so it will not split.
6. **The burial stakes,** each dead stake-hand marked by a stake driven without the word so it will go down.

**Who is here.** Two or three old stake-hands who would not leave, perhaps; they would be the last with the whole stake-word. Harrow eels in the stake-yards.

**Old magic.** **Staking**, at its source: a stake driven with the word holds where it should not, for years, and then fails all at once when the word is forgotten. Driving with the word costs the driver warmth; stake-hands drive in pairs, one to drive and one to keep the fire.

**Wonder and mystery.** The burial stakes sinking slowly, a graveyard going down into the fen at the speed of a life.

**Dangers.** The dry ways (II, V in water). Harrow eels (II).

**Finds.** Seasoned oak in the stake-yards. Beetles and marking cord. The word, if anyone is left to give it.

**Hooks.** Find the last stake-hands. Bring one to re-drive the Nine Stakes' rotten boundary stakes.

### Tavin's road station

*Ruined road station · on the road south to the Downs, about 10 miles past Dunmere · empty · danger II · lore names, details (new)*

**At a glance.** A Crown post on the road south: a stone stable, a good well and a keeper's house, with a sign over the well that reads *TAVIN KEPT THIS. KEEP IT.*

**History.** Last kept by a man named Tavin until S.2. Nobody knows what became of him. His well is still good, and travellers keep it as the sign asks.

**How it looks.** A roofless keeper's house, a stable with half a roof, and the well with a new rope on it: someone always replaces the rope.

**Layout.** 1. The road and the milestone. 2. The keeper's house, roofless. 3. The stable, half-roofed, where travellers sleep. 4. The well and its sign. 5. The keeper's garden, gone to nettles, with one apple tree that still fruits.

**Who is here.** Whoever is using the station for a night. Greymaw on the Downs road.

**Old magic.** None.

**Wonder and mystery.** The well rope, always new. Who replaces it.

**Dangers.** Greymaw (II, III).

**Finds.** Good water. Apples in autumn. In the keeper's house hearth, a hidden box (new): Tavin's Crown seal, his road-book of every traveller to S.2, and a letter to a daughter in the Downs.

**Hooks.** Deliver Tavin's letter. Keep the well.

### Alder Fold

*Abandoned holdings · beyond Tavin's road station, in an alder carr · empty since the first autumn · danger II · lore names, details (new)*

**At a glance.** Three neighbouring farmsteads in an alder carr whose households left for Dunmere in the first autumn and never came back: doors shut, a wedding blanket still on a bed, a dog's bowl by a step.

**History.** The first refugees to reach Dunmere came from here, asking for one night inside the walls (`../dunmere-first-arrivals.md`). They left in a hurry. Garran Vey's son **Oren**, twenty, was told to follow the tracks only as far as the old charcoal path, and did not come back; this charcoal path is the alder-burners' path through the carr, not the Bell Road's lodge.

**How it looks.** Three low farmhouses under red-barked alders, their thatch green, their doors shut. Everything inside as it was left on a single morning thirty years ago.

**Layout.** 1. The carr track. 2. The Vey house, the Renn house and the Senn house. 3. The shared yard and its dog's bowl. 4. The old charcoal path, going off into the carr. 5. A burners' clearing at the path's end (new), with a cold clamp and a pair of boots.

**Who is here.** Nobody. Greymaw in the carr.

**Old magic.** None.

**Wonder and mystery.** A morning thirty years long. The boots at the end of the charcoal path.

**Dangers.** Greymaw (II, III). The carr's sinks.

**Finds.** The wedding blanket. Mera Vey's hen-house, with its brass hen-bell. Kitchen knives, a scythe blade, a quern. The boots, which may be Oren's.

**Hooks.** What became of Oren Vey. Bring something home to the families in Dunmere.

### Moss Hythe

*The cutters' banks · the high fen about 7 miles north of the Watchhouse · one woman, Brede, in the gang's turf hut · danger II, V on the moss · lore, with the layout (new)*

**At a glance.** The peat banks of the high fen: long black cut faces, old stacks gone grey, and one turf hut beside the last stack, dry peat enough to keep the Watchhouse hearth all winter, kept by a woman who will not give it to a refuge that argues about letting coughers in.

**History.** A gang of six cut here most of their lives. Amis Brough, the gang's eldest, went through a skin of moss into a sink in S.24, and three more died of the cough. Brede is the last of them here. Her sibling, the Peat-cutter background, is at the Outside Fire.

**How it looks.** A black step-cut slope of peat above the fen, the cut faces striped brown and black, the moss between them green-gold and treacherous. One turf hut with smoke, and beside it a stack the size of a cottage.

**Layout.** 1. The track up from the Watchhouse. 2. The cut banks. 3. The hut and the last stack. 4. The sink, marked by a slane left standing in the moss. 5. The cutters' cairn (new), where the gang left a turf for what lives in the water.

**Who is here.** **Brede** (see `../people/the-fen.md`).

**Old magic.** None. The turf at the cairn does nothing, and the cutters leave it anyway.

**Wonder and mystery.** Bog-oak in the cut faces, black trunks older than the kingdom. The slane standing in the moss over Amis's sink.

**Dangers.** The moss (V where it skins a sink). The cutters' chest.

**Finds.** The last stack, 23 kg to a carry. Bog-oak. Brede's spare slane. Amis's slane, if you are fool enough to go for it.

**Hooks.** Bring Brede in, which means getting the door opened to a cougher; or take the peat and leave her; or leave both.

### Reedwake

*A lost town of moorings in the west fen · about twelve miles south of Saint Orra's Steps, under its lamp · abandoned, visited by punt · danger II · name and the return-bread custom from the archive; the rest (new)*

**At a glance.** A reed-cutters' town that lived on the water and was left on it: a mere full of black mooring posts in rows, the sunk hulls of reed-barges between them, a boat-shed on its one island and a chapel built on a grounded hull. On some of the posts there is still bread.

**History.** Reedwake cut reed for the whole west fen: thatch, matting, rushlights, and fodder in the hard winters. Its people lived as much on their barges as in houses, and when someone went out on the water a loaf was baked and set on their mooring post, the **return-bread**, to be eaten when they came back. The archive's Reedwake chapter is lost, so the rest of this history is (new). When the water rose after S.0, the town moved onto its barges and lashed them together in the mere. The freeze of S.4 split the hulls where they lay. The last families went to Saint Orra's Steps and Dunmere by S.7, and they still keep the custom there.

**How it looks.** Flat open water with posts standing in it in rows, hundreds of them, iron-banded and black, each carved with a family mark. On some there is a flat stone where a loaf once sat. On still days the sunk hulls show under the surface between the rows. The reed beds have run wild and stand taller than a man on every side. At night the lamp on Saint Orra's Steps is a point of light on the northern edge of the sky.

**Getting there.** Less than a day by punt from Saint Orra's Steps (12 miles). On foot from the Watchhouse it is 26 miles of off-road fen, three days or more, and nobody does it on foot.

**Layout.**

1. **The cut,** the channel in through the reed beds, marked at its mouth by a leaning post with an empty bell bracket.
2. **The Rows,** the mooring posts, standing in lines like a drowned forest. Each row was a family's, and the marks say whose.
3. **The sunk barges,** between the rows, their decks a hand's depth under the water. They can be walked in summer if you know them.
4. **The boat-shed island,** the only roofed building: a long reed-thatched shed on a mound, with the cutters' sickles still racked inside and a slip for building punts.
5. **The chapel barge,** a chapel built on a hull and run aground on the mud, listing to one side. It is dedicated to **Saint Wenna the Ferrywoman** (new link to Dunmere's saint). On its walls are painted oars, one for each of the town's people who went out and did not come home.
6. **The bread posts,** a cluster of posts by the chapel barge where the stones are kept clean.

**Who is here.** Nobody lives here. Reed-cutters from Saint Orra's Steps come by punt in late summer for the wild reed. Harrow eels in the sunk hulls. Gallows herons on the posts. And whoever leaves the bread.

**Old magic. Keeping:** the chapel barge's threshold still holds. It was blessed as a house, and the Keeping seems not to care that the house floats; nobody knows why. The return-bread is a custom and works no magic.

**Wonder and mystery.** Some posts still get bread: a few fresh loaves each winter, on the posts of families everyone thought were gone. Nobody knows who rows out with them, whether a survivor living somewhere in the reeds, a cutter from the Steps keeping faith, or someone who wants Reedwake to look lived in. The painted oars, which list the town's lost by name.

**Dangers.** The sunk decks (II): slick, and one step off them is deep cold water. Harrow eels in the hulls (II). Winter ice on the mere that looks firm and is not.

**Finds.** Reed sickles and whetstones in the boat-shed. Seasoned punt timber. The family marks on the posts, the only surviving record of every household in the town. The names on the painted oars.

**Hooks.** Follow the bread and find out who brings it. A family mark on a post that matches someone at the Watchhouse. In The Long Account, a punt-only site; a loaf left on a bread post is a quiet way for players to mark someone missing.

### The Nailhouse

*A smithy village on a bog-iron island · about nine miles south-south-west of the White Trees, a day's spur off the Salt Trace · about twenty people · danger I · name, the scrap forging and the slag-iron claim from the lore; the rest (new)*

**At a glance.** The one place in the east fen that still makes iron things, though it cannot make iron: a forge house roofed with nail-plates on a low island over flooded ore pits, where the Haskett smiths forge the fen's scrap back into nails, rims and spear-heads and count every nail.

**History.** Bog iron was dug and smelted here long before the King's roads, in clay furnaces whose slag built the island up out of the fen. When cheaper upland iron came in, the furnaces went cold, but the smiths stayed to work what the carters brought. After S.0 the pits flooded and could not be dug again. Since then the Nailhouse has done what the whole fen does with iron: it cannot make it, only move it. Wet ash eats iron, and scrap comes in orange. The Haskett family have kept the forge for four generations (new).

**How it looks.** A low island of old slag heaps grown over with moss, with black glassy stones in every path. The forge house is squat and stone-built, its roof a scale-pattern of iron nail-plates riveted edge over edge, rust-red where it is not greased black. Smoke goes up from it all day. Where the pits were, there are three ponds of orange water.

**Getting there.** A day's walk (9 miles) on the carters' spur that leaves the Salt Trace at the White Trees. Stakeby is 13 miles west across the drowned middle, by the dry ways only. Brackenreach is about 27 miles north-north-east.

**Layout.**

1. **The landing,** where the carters' spur comes onto the island over a slag causeway.
2. **The scrap yard,** where carters trade bent nails, hinge plates and chain links, weighed on a beam scale.
3. **The forge house,** two hearths and a bellows worked by a treadle, under the nail-plate roof.
4. **The nail-loft,** above the forge, where every nail is counted and tallied by the hundred. At the Nailhouse a nail is money.
5. **The slag heaps,** where the smiths pick out slag-iron.
6. **The bog-iron pits,** three, flooded and orange. The deepest has never been sounded.
7. **The houses,** four of them, at the island's high end.

**Who is here.** About twenty people. **Hulda Haskett**, the eldest smith, who will not sell a spear-head to anyone she has not fed first. **Ware Haskett**, her son, who works the treadle and keeps the count. **Agna Haskett**, who goes out on the carts to the White Trees. Carters of the Cinder Factors, who come and go.

**Old magic.** None. The smiths swear that slag-iron rusts slower than common iron. Nobody has tested it fairly, and everyone who owns slag-iron has a reason to say so.

**Wonder and mystery.** Things come up out of the deepest pit when the smiths rake it for ore: a spear-head older than any road, a bronze ring. The peat keeps what falls into it, and the fen has had a long time to drop things.

**Dangers.** Rustlings, drawn to the scrap yard (I to II). The pits, cold and deep. The smiths, if you try to leave with a nail you have not paid for.

**Finds.** Nails, rims and spear-heads, the cheapest honest iron in the fen. Slag-iron. Greased tools. Bent nails, which people buy to leave at roadside shrines.

**Hooks.** Bring scrap and trade it for iron. The count in the nail-loft as a running economy. Something old raked up from the deepest pit. In The Long Account, the fen's iron sink: scrap goes in and goods come out.

### Bellwether Yard

*A drovers' fair-ground on the fen's firm northern edge · about twelve miles west-south-* *west of the Watchhouse by the old drove · empty most of the year, held by a few drovers in summer · danger II · name from the archive; the rest (new)*

**At a glance.** A great ring of stone pens on the last firm ground before the reeds, with a drovers' hall and a bare bell post in the middle, where the flocks of the Bell Road uplands were once brought down to sell to the fen. A few drovers still come in summer, and they carry bells.

**History.** For centuries the upland flocks came down the Bell Road every autumn, past the Watchhouse, and along the drove to the Yard, where the fen's buyers met them. The fair paid for the stone pens, the hall and an inn that has gone. Its name comes from the bell-wether, the lead sheep that wears the bell. After S.0 the uplands lost most of their sheep and the fen lost most of its buyers. The autumn fair stopped in S.3. That year the Yard's own bell was taken down from its post and sold for its metal, and nobody now remembers to whom.

**How it looks.** A low dry rise of short turf, with the reeds starting at its edge. Dry-stone pens in rings, one inside another, closed with hurdle gates. In the middle an oak post twice a man's height, with an empty iron yoke at the top. The hall is long and low, its roof half fallen in.

**Getting there.** About 12 miles west-south-west of the Watchhouse along the drove: a day on foot, a day and a half with a flock. Moss Hythe is 12 miles east. Saint Orra's Steps is 16 miles south-west across open fen.

**Layout.**

1. **The drove,** a walled track in from the Watchhouse, wide enough for a flock.
2. **The great ring,** the outer pens, for holding the flocks.
3. **The sale pens,** the inner ring, numbered on their gateposts.
4. **The bell post,** and the bargaining ground round it.
5. **The drovers' hall,** roofed at one end, with a hearth and benches carved with drovers' marks and the names of their dogs.
6. **The wash,** a stone tank where sheep were washed before sale, now a pond.
7. **The strays' fold,** at the fen edge where the turf gives out.

**Who is here.** Empty most of the year. In summer, three to six drovers with the last flocks: **Garth Collier**, who has walked the Bell Road for forty years, and **Tam**, his granddaughter, with their dogs. Buyers walk out from the Watchhouse and Moss Hythe. Goblins come to the strays' fold at night.

**Old magic. Carrying.** The drovers say a bell-wether's bell cast at Hob's Height will bring a flock home through fog. It seems to be true, and nobody understands it: whether the bell's sound carries further than it should, or the flock simply follows the one sound it knows. The sheep work no magic; the bell does what Hob's Height bells do. Drovers who ring through a long fog come home colder than the weather can explain.

**Wonder and mystery.** The empty yoke on the bell post. Where the Yard bell went. The wether bells themselves, and whether the drovers know more about them than they say.

**Dangers.** Fog on the fen edge (II). Goblins at the strays' fold (II). The reeds beyond the turf, where a stray sheep and the person chasing it both get lost.

**Finds.** Wether bells, rare and costly. Hurdles, shears, a drovers' tally-stick. The dog names carved in the hall benches.

**Hooks.** The summer fair as an event. A wether bell to buy, or to steal. The Yard's lost bell turning up somewhere it should not be. In The Long Account, a seasonal market: open in summer, empty and dangerous the rest of the year.

### The Nine Stakes

*A peat-cutters' village in the south fen · about ten miles east-north-east of Merewick, at the head of the bargain road · about thirty people · danger II · name, the Merewick trade and Odo Starke from the lore; the rest (new)*

**At a glance.** A cutters' village inside a ring of nine boundary stakes that mark its peat rights, with drying racks, a row of turf houses and a flat stone where every bargain is sworn. It lives on peat and on the road to Merewick, and that road is dangerous because everyone can see what you carry.

**History.** The village's peat rights come from a charter it no longer has (new). What it has instead are the nine stakes, oak driven deep by Stakeby's stake-hands in the old way, re-driven every generation, each one bounding a family's cutting. After S.0 the cutters went on cutting, because peat is fuel and fuel is the fen's other coin. Flour comes from Merewick's mill one way and peat and reeds go the other, with bargains made on both. Since the tower fell in the autumn of S.29 and Stakeby broke up, nobody has been able to re-drive a stake, and two of the nine have rotted. This autumn **Odo Starke**, a cutter of the Nine Stakes, went out with a cart and did not come back.

**How it looks.** Flat brown country cut into steps where the peat has been taken, with black water standing in the cuttings. Nine tall stakes, iron-capped, each notched with the mark of the family whose cutting it bounds. Racks of drying turves like low walls. Smoke from the turf houses, which smells like the whole fen burning slowly.

**Getting there.** About 10 miles west-south-west to Merewick by the bargain road, a day with a cart. The Watchhouse is about 27 miles north, three days off road. Low Harrow is 19 miles east-north-east across the drowned middle.

**Layout.**

1. **The nine stakes,** in a ring about a mile round. Seven are sound and two are rotten.
2. **The cutters' row,** turf-roofed houses on a spine of firm ground.
3. **The drying racks,** where turves stand to dry from spring to autumn.
4. **The cuttings,** the stepped workings, deep and wet.
5. **The bargain stone,** at the end of the row: flat and worn. A trade sworn there with both hands on the stone is binding, by custom.
6. **The road's head,** where carts load for Merewick.

**Who is here.** About thirty people. **Dowsett Starke**, Odo's brother, who keeps the bargain stone and the count of whose turn it is. **Mattock**, the oldest cutter, who remembers the last re-driving of the stakes. The cutters' families.

**Old magic.** None. The bargain stone holds by custom alone, which in the Nine Stakes is stronger than law.

**Wonder and mystery.** Odo Starke. The village does not know he is dead: his name-strip asked whoever found him to lime him. Who the third traveller at his cart was, and whether the three came to the Watchhouse door first, nobody knows. The two rotten stakes, and who now has the right to the peat they bounded.

**Dangers.** The cuttings, with deep cold water at the bottom of every step (II). The Merewick road, where visible cargo attracts claims (II). Arguments at the bargain stone that end in the cuttings.

**Finds.** Peat turves, the fen's fuel. Cutters' spades. Flour from Merewick.

**Hooks.** Carry word of Odo, and his name-strip, back to the Nine Stakes. Find a stake-hand, one of Edda's scattered people, to re-drive the rotten stakes. A bargain sworn at the stone that a player cannot keep. In The Long Account, the fen's fuel market.
