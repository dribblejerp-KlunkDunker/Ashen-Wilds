# The Chapel of the Kept Hand

## Document status

Project: Ashen Wilds. Location: the private chapel in [Halwen House](halwen-house.md), above [The Last Gardens](the-last-gardens.md). Preserved from the worldbuilding conversation of 2026-11-01. The full chapel write-up follows. This is proposed lore, not implemented content or verified supernatural mechanics. The proposed origin of the face coverings is author-facing history; present-day residents still disagree about it. This addition does not modify existing lore, character assets, or development checkpoints.

The chapel stands against Halwen House’s uphill wall, joined to the family passage by a narrow door. Its outer entrance opens onto a paved court where servants once waited to be admitted.

Over that entrance, a carved hand closes around a small flame.

The fingers have been polished by generations of people touching them before entering. The flame is chipped.

Inside, the air smells of cold mortar, old cloth, and animal nesting. There is enough roof left to keep the central aisle dry.

Nine seated people could fit comfortably.

The household used to send twenty.

Everything below is proposed lore expanding the chapel already established at Halwen House.

## 1. What the chapel was for

The Halwens dedicated the chapel to Saint Edras of the Kept Hand, a local saint of the Keeping.

According to household tradition, Edras held open a burning storehouse door until the people inside escaped. His hand was so badly burned that its fingers healed closed.

Later images showed him holding a flame.

The distinction was useful to the family. A saint who had suffered to preserve an escape became the patron of a household that expected obedience.

The chapel’s central word was **protection**.

People came here for naming rites, marriages, household appointments, witnessed agreements, and private mourning. It was also where servants were reminded that refusing a lawful duty endangered more than their employment.

The household chaplain could make an instruction feel binding long after the person giving it had left the room.

## 2. The room itself

The chapel is built of heavy stone, with a timber roof partly sheltered by the house and the records tower.

Its survival is uneven. The altar end remains dry. Water enters along the outer wall and collects beneath the servants’ benches.

```text
                    UPHILL WALL
          [Stone altar]   [Covenant recess]
                  |
            Family kneelers
                  |
         Low rail and narrow gate
                  |
             Central aisle
                  |
     Servants’ benches — Memorial wall
                  |
             Outer entrance
                  |
               Paved court

 Family passage enters beside the kneelers.
 Tower stair is reached outside the chapel.
```

The furnishings make the old hierarchy visible without anyone explaining it.

The family had padded kneelers close to the altar.

The servants sat on plain benches.

Several stood at the back, beside the entrance where cold air entered whenever someone arrived late.

### The low rail

A waist-high rail divides the two sections.

It was not locked during ordinary worship. Servants could pass through when invited.

The distinction mattered because invitation could be withdrawn.

Part of the rail has been sawn away for timber. Two uprights remain, with a pale rectangular scar where a hand used to rest while opening the gate.

## 3. The covered faces

Five carved figures occupy shallow niches.

Saint Edras stands at the center. The others depict lesser household patrons: a woman carrying a water vessel, a watcher with a shuttered lamp, a kneeling soldier, and a child holding an adult’s empty glove.

Their faces are covered.

Not their whole heads—only the eyes, noses, and mouths.

Each covering is a strip of cloth tied behind the carving.

### How it began

For the chapel’s proposed history, the first face was covered during the winter after the Halwens left.

The people still occupying the house gathered to ask whether the remaining stores should be opened.

The chaplain had gone south. No one felt entitled to conduct a service.

A kitchen servant named Hessa Pell took off her headcloth and tied it over Edras’s face.

She said:

> “He needn’t watch us ask again.”

They opened the stores that afternoon.

Someone covered the other figures later.

### What survives now

The cloth is not all original.

Some strips have rotted and been replaced. Nobody at the Gardens openly admits to maintaining them.

Older residents offer different explanations:

- The saints should not have to witness what happened here.
- The saints witnessed it and did nothing.
- Covered faces cannot recognize those who enter.
- Hessa merely wanted the carved eyes turned away while people took food.

These remain beliefs and interpretations, not established supernatural rules.

Removing the coverings reveals ordinary stone faces.

The consequences would come first from whoever notices.

## 4. The household covenant

The covenant occupies a plastered recess beside the altar.

Its lettering was once renewed every few years. The upper lines retain traces of blue pigment and a narrow border of painted grain.

The text recorded duties in both directions.

### What the Halwens promised

The surviving passage can be reconstructed as:

> We shall maintain shelter against the season, provision against want, and refuge against violence for those entered beneath our hand.

### What the household promised

Below it:

> We shall render faithful service, preserve the house, conceal no due portion, and answer when called.

The lower passage has been scratched repeatedly.

Some marks are deep enough to expose the stone beneath the plaster.

The upper promise is almost untouched.

### Why the damage is selective

People did not necessarily reject the covenant.

Some rejected being held to their part after the Halwens abandoned theirs.

Leaving the upper words intact preserved the accusation.

A visitor who assumes the scratched portion is simple vandalism misses what the room is saying.

### The easily missed line

Close to the bottom, beneath damage and grime, is a smaller instruction:

> Let each obligation be spoken in the hearing of those it binds.

This gives the chapel a practical connection to the register stories: witnessing was supposed to constrain authority, not merely authenticate it.

It does not prove that everyone heard every agreement—or that witnesses could safely object.

## 5. The altar

The altar is a single stone slab on a masonry base.

Its corners are worn where people steadied themselves while kneeling or rising.

A shallow depression near the front held a small ceremonial lamp. The lamp is gone.

There is no concealed treasury inside the altar.

Someone has already tested that possibility. A section of the base has been broken open and crudely replaced.

### What remains behind it

Between the altar and the wall lies a narrow accumulation of objects:

- Two corroded pins.
- A small wooden bead.
- A folded piece of darkened cloth.
- The broken handle of a child’s cup.
- Several bent nails.

Some fell there accidentally.

Others were pushed into the gap deliberately, beyond easy reach.

The folded cloth contains nothing valuable. Its significance, if any, depends on someone recognizing it.

### A recent intrusion

One bent nail is cleaner than the others.

It has been wrapped with pale thread.

This establishes that someone has used the chapel recently. It does not establish what they asked for.

## 6. The memorial wall

Along the servants’ side, small names have been cut into the stone.

They do not resemble the family’s formal memorial tablets.

The letters are shallow, uneven, and crowded where later hands tried to add names without cutting through earlier ones.

Some include a trade:

> MERET — LAUNDRY
> OLD TAV — CARTER

Others have only an initial.

### The first names

The earliest additions commemorate household dependents who died during the first winters after the Retreat.

Later visitors added people who had worked here before the catastrophe but never received a place in the family’s written memorials.

This turned the wall into an unofficial household record.

It is neither complete nor necessarily accurate.

### A surviving disagreement

One name has been struck through.

Beside it, another hand has written:

> HE CAME BACK

Someone else has cut beneath that:

> NOT FOR US

No document in the chapel resolves the dispute.

A living witness might.

## 7. The family tablets

The Halwens’ dead occupy the opposite wall.

Their memorials have dates, titles, marriages, and phrases describing generosity.

The stonework is finer than anything on the servants’ side.

One tablet praises a former head of the house for maintaining the lower waterworks during a year of scarcity.

Another commemorates a child who died young.

That child’s stone has not been defaced.

The adult memorial beside it has been struck with a hammer.

These distinctions matter. The people who damaged the chapel were capable of choosing what to spare.

### The empty place

A prepared recess remains without a tablet.

It was intended for the family member who owned the estate when the Smothering came.

He went south.

No one here knows where he was buried.

## 8. The chaplain’s cupboard

A narrow oak cupboard is set into the wall near the family passage.

Its doors have been forced, but the frame remains.

Most valuable ritual objects went with the departing household. The remnants are practical and incomplete:

- A cracked basin.
- A bundle of candle ends fused together.
- A damaged book of household rites.
- Plain cloth used during services.
- Several wooden witness tokens.

### The witness tokens

Each token is a small piece of wood marked with the open hand.

During certain agreements, witnesses held them while the terms were spoken. The tokens were returned afterward.

They were not proof by themselves.

That limitation has been forgotten—or ignored—by some people who possess them.

Someone could carry one south and claim to have witnessed an agreement here.

Someone else could recognize that its mark differs from the tokens used in the year being claimed.

The object opens a dispute. It does not settle one.

### The damaged rites book

The book is swollen and incomplete.

Its most usable sections concern naming, burial, and the reception of a person into a household.

The pages setting out the duties of the head of household have been removed.

Whether the chaplain took them, a survivor preserved them, or somebody destroyed them remains unresolved.

## 9. The last service

Give the chapel one specific event people remember differently.

During the evacuation, the household gathered here before the final wagons departed.

The chaplain spoke a blessing over the journey.

Servants expected their arrangements to be announced afterward.

Instead, the steward read a list of people required to remain: gardeners, maintenance hands, two kitchen workers, and those judged necessary to secure the property.

The family left through the inner passage.

Those staying behind were dismissed through the outer entrance.

For years afterward, people argued about whether the chaplain knew the promised return would never happen.

The chapel cannot answer that question.

It can show which door each group used.

## 10. Who visits now

The room should have several present-day users whose purposes overlap without forming an organized cult.

### Nera Halwen

Nera occasionally enters to inspect damp near the garden office wall.

She says the chapel offers the safest way through.

She also stops at the covenant.

She does not kneel.

### An older gardener

One resident replaces a face covering when it falls apart.

They will not explain the practice publicly.

If challenged, they say the chapel should not be left untended merely because its owners were faithless.

### Sabeth Rill

The hidden occupant upstairs uses the chapel’s family passage when weather makes the outer route unpleasant.

She has recently left a small offering behind the altar.

It may be the thread-wrapped nail.

Her reasons can remain private unless trust develops between her and the player.

### Passing travelers

A few people shelter here without approaching the Gardens.

Some leave marks showing arrival and departure dates.

One has written a destination beside a name.

No departure is recorded.

## 11. A scene that belongs here

A returning religious official offers to hold a service for the Gardens.

He brings clean candles and a proper lamp.

Several residents want him to proceed.

Others refuse to enter while the covenant’s upper promise remains unacknowledged.

The official asks that the saints’ faces be uncovered before worship.

An elderly woman says the cloth must stay.

Neither person begins by threatening anyone.

The dispute becomes difficult when children start removing the coverings because an adult has told them to help prepare the room.

Now the player has something immediate to interrupt or allow.

The meaningful choices are not simply belief versus unbelief:

- Hold the service with the coverings intact.
- Read the entire covenant aloud before removing them.
- Move the gathering outside.
- Let separate groups use the chapel at different times.
- Refuse the service.
- Allow the official to proceed despite objections.

None of these choices establishes which interpretation of the saints is correct.

Each establishes who is permitted to decide what happens in the room.

## 12. Shelter without safety

The chapel is useful because much of its floor remains dry.

It is dangerous because its exits are unequal.

The outer door is obvious.

The family passage is narrow, partly obstructed, and unfamiliar to most travelers.

A person sleeping beside the altar may hear someone entering the court but fail to notice movement behind the inner door.

If the player clears the passage, secures the doors, and checks the roof, the chapel can become a genuinely better shelter.

That improvement should remain.

It still needs fuel. It still advertises occupation through light and smoke. It still stands within a property claimed by people who do not live there.

## 13. The image to leave with

When the room is quiet, the coverings move slightly in the draft.

The saints appear to breathe because the cloth lifts and settles over their mouths.

A closer inspection finds an open joint in the wall beside the family passage.

The explanation is ordinary.

It does not make sleeping beneath them much easier.

Across the aisle, the household covenant remains legible above the scratches:

> Refuge against violence for those entered beneath our hand.

Someone has placed a sleeping blanket directly below it.
