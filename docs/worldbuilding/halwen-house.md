# Halwen House

## Document status

Project: Ashen Wilds. Region: Last Gardens, southern Bell Road, Blackfen. Preserved from the worldbuilding conversation of 2026-11-01. The full location write-up follows. This is proposed lore, not implemented content or a finalized engine map. Related documents: [The Last Gardens](the-last-gardens.md) and [The Chapel of the Kept Hand](halwen-house-private-chapel.md). This addition does not modify existing lore, character assets, or development checkpoints. Revised 2026-11-05 for owner decision O1 (only humans are people). The original is in git history at commit fd60061.

Halwen House should be the first substantial ruin near the Last Gardens: close enough that its inhabitants know its silhouette by heart, dangerous enough that they still postpone going inside.

It was not a palace. It was the country residence of a moderately powerful family whose wealth came from land, storage rights, and a share in traffic along the Bell Road.

Its owners could afford glasshouses, a private chapel, and servants’ stairs.

They could also afford to leave.

The house’s defining feature is **separation**. Family and servants used different doors, stairs, water stores, and sleeping quarters. Thirty years after the Retreat, those divisions still determine which rooms can be reached, what can be recovered, and who is likely to die trying.

Everything below is proposed lore expanding the existing location.

## 1. The approach

The old carriage drive leaves the road below the gardens, climbs behind a belt of trees, and turns toward the house from the side.

It is longer than the servants’ path.

That was deliberate. Guests arrived through an avenue of pollarded trees, not past the kitchen refuse and laundry yard.

Now the avenue is a tunnel of neglected growth. Several trunks have split. Fallen branches make the carriage entrance difficult for a loaded handcart.

The servants’ path remains usable because the gardeners still take it when salvaging stone.

### What comes into view

The house presents three unequal parts:

- A two-storey main block with a steep roof, much of it now open.
- A lower service wing extending downhill.
- A walled garden and former glasshouse range along the sunnier side of the slope.

A narrow tower rises beside the private chapel. It held a small bell and an upper records room, not defensive battlements.

The bell is gone.

Its mounting beam remains visible through the opening.

### The first warning

The main entrance stands open, but the front steps are littered with slate fragments.

Some are old and furred with dirt.

Others have sharp, clean edges.

A person paying attention can tell that material is still falling.

## 2. How the estate withdrew

The Halwens did not depart in a panicked single night.

First, the youngest children and their attendants were sent south.

Then went portable silver, correspondence, money, and selected household goods. The family’s final wagons carried bedding and food while servants remained to guard what could not be moved.

The steward issued instructions for continued work.

Garden beds were to be maintained. Accounts were to be kept. No standing timber was to be cut without authorization.

For several weeks, people obeyed.

Then the kitchen stores ran short.

The last household servants moved down to the cottages in stages. They brought useful pots, blankets, tools, and eventually the contents of the seed room.

The house was stripped unevenly afterward.

Rooms people could reach safely lost almost everything. Rooms behind jammed doors or damaged floors sometimes retained ordinary objects—not because those objects were worthless, but because removing them required more labor and risk than anyone could spare.

## 3. The layout

This is a working spatial arrangement, not a finalized engine map.

```text
                         UPHILL

              Private chapel — Records tower
                     |                |
               Family passage — Upper stair
                     |
        Drawing room — Entrance hall — Dining room
                              |
                         Service door
                              |
             Pantry — Kitchen — Scullery
                         |
                 Servants’ stair
                         |
                Laundry / service yard
                         |
               Garden walls and paths
                         |
                  LAST GARDENS

        Glasshouse range lies beside the service wing.
```

The important feature is the downhill service wing. Its lower rooms sit beneath the level of the main entrance and connect to yards, drains, and garden passages.

That creates several ways through the property without turning it into a maze of arbitrary secret corridors.

## 4. The entrance hall

The floor was once patterned with dark and pale stone.

Most of the pale slabs have been lifted. Their rectangular beds collect ash and rainwater.

The staircase remains, but its outer railing has been removed for wood. The landing above is stained where water enters through the roof.

A large household emblem hangs over the inner doorway: a hand holding a sheaf.

Someone has chipped away the fingers.

### What remains

The hall contains things too awkward to carry:

- A cracked stone bench.
- An overturned iron-bound chest with its lock broken out.
- A wardrobe carcass stripped of doors.
- A large framed portrait with the painted face cut away.

The chest is empty.

It should not conceal a second treasure compartment. Its importance is that someone opened it long ago and left the evidence.

### A navigational clue

On the underside of the remaining stair rail, someone scratched:

> USE BACK STAIR

The warning is useful, but old.

It does not certify that the back stair remains safe.

## 5. The dining room

The dining table has been cut in half.

One half is gone.

The other remains wedged beneath a fallen ceiling beam, its legs driven into the floorboards by the weight.

The room is colder than the hall because one wall has opened toward the garden.

### What can be recovered

The surviving table timber is good.

Recovering it requires supporting the beam first. Attempting to drag it free should produce visible movement, falling dust, and cracking before collapse—not an unforeseeable punishment.

Other useful finds include wall brackets, a damaged serving trolley, and the brass sleeves from a curtain pole.

### What tells the story

A narrow cabinet still holds several chipped cups.

The valuable pieces left with the household. The servants used what remained until they stopped coming up from the cottages.

One cup contains hardened candle grease.

Beside it lies a spoon worn thin at the edge.

People ate here after the dining room ceased to be a dining room.

## 6. The kitchen and garden office

This is the most thoroughly used part of the ruin.

The range has been broken for iron. The large copper vessels are gone. Shelves have been dismantled where they could be reached.

The chimney breast remains sound enough to offer shelter from weather, although no one should assume its flue is clear.

### The garden office

A narrow room beside the kitchen held planting notes, supply accounts, tools awaiting repair, and estate plans.

Its outer wall is damp. The door has swollen against the floor.

Inside, much of the paper has become a fused mass.

Enough survives to be useful.

Possible discoveries include:

- A plan showing the main garden drains.
- A note identifying which glasshouse bed was filled over an older channel.
- A list of seed varieties once stored on the estate.
- A repair sketch for the lower cistern access.
- A household order forbidding servants from drawing from the family’s reserved water store.

These are practical fragments, not one conveniently intact master plan.

Nera would recognize abbreviations and old plot names that the player might not.

### A smaller object

A set of wooden labels hangs on a nail.

Several have been scraped and reused so often that the original writing is nearly gone.

One is marked *winter cabbage* on one side and *lamp oil* on the other.

## 7. The water system

The house should clarify the Gardens’ investigation rather than introduce impossible plumbing.

Halwen House stands uphill. Water is not secretly flowing uphill from the Gardens’ cistern into a bedroom.

The estate originally maintained several connected but distinct features:

- Roof collection at the house.
- A small upper storage tank.
- Covered channels carrying water downhill.
- Garden distribution points.
- The larger lower cistern now used by the settlement.
- Separate drains carrying excess water away.

Over time, sections were blocked, diverted, or abandoned.

The residents maintain the portions they can reach. They do not possess a complete understanding of everything beneath the property.

### A strong underlying explanation

For the proposed water-shortage arc, use several contributing problems:

1. A damaged feeder channel is diverting some incoming water before it reaches the lower cistern.
2. A failed seam in the cistern lining is allowing stored water to escape.
3. Concealed washing has increased consumption.

This lets different residents be partly right.

Finding the hidden washing matters. It does not repair the seam.

Finding the estate plan matters. It does not perform the excavation.

### What the house provides

The garden office can reveal where to inspect.

A service passage can give access to part of the feeder channel.

Neither should eliminate the need for work in the settlement courtyard.

## 8. The glasshouse range

Most of the glass is gone.

What remains lies in the soil, hidden among weeds and old ash.

Low walls preserve the shapes of planting beds. Iron uprights lean over them. Along the back wall runs a narrow maintenance trench.

Some sections of trench are covered by stone slabs.

Others are covered only by accumulated debris.

### Why people still enter

The range contains recoverable fittings, short lengths of pipe, brackets, and useful stone.

Its sheltered beds may also contain surviving plants or roots worth identifying, but not a miraculous crop capable of feeding the district.

### The danger

The range is a poor place to fight.

Footing is uncertain. Broken glass makes crawling or falling costly. The narrow beds and trench complicate retreat.

A small household of road waifs squatting there, the people others call the Gutter Kin, could make it troublesome without controlling the entire estate. Their presence should leave recognizable evidence: disturbed soil, fresh refuse, missing metal, and narrow routes through the wall.

Driving them out would make salvage safer.

It would not repair the house.

## 9. The private chapel

The chapel is intact enough to feel different from the stripped domestic rooms.

Its fittings were less useful than kitchen iron and sound timber. Some people also refused to remove them.

A stone altar stands beneath a roof stained by damp.

The carved figures around it have had their faces covered with scraps of cloth.

Nobody at the Gardens agrees who did that.

### The household covenant

A shallow wall recess holds the remains of a painted statement of duties.

Enough survives to distinguish two promises:

The household was to maintain its dependents.

Its dependents were to serve faithfully.

The lower portion has been repeatedly scratched.

The upper portion is almost untouched.

### What the chapel offers

It is a reasonably dry room with an outward-opening door.

That makes it tempting shelter.

It is also visible from the approach and has only one easy exit. Anyone staying there should understand the tradeoff.

No curse is required to make the place dangerous.

## 10. The records tower

The tower stair is narrow and enclosed.

Unlike the main house, it retains much of its roof. The upper room is relatively dry.

Its contents were selected for removal during the Retreat, but not every document went south.

What remains includes empty shelves, discarded wrappers, damaged duplicate accounts, and records someone judged unimportant.

### What might matter now

A nursery inventory may identify bedding taken to a cottage.

A wage account may establish who worked at the estate.

A boundary sketch may contradict a later claimant’s map.

An unsigned draft may promise something that was never formally granted.

Not every document has legal force. Not every genuine record is true.

### Physical risk

One stair tread has broken near the top.

A person carrying a heavy chest cannot simply step around it.

The intact room above can tempt scavengers into taking more than the stairs will bear.

## 11. The upstairs bedroom

This is the room already glimpsed in the location outline.

Its bedding has been folded. Ash has been cleared from the sill. Small quantities of food appear and disappear.

For the location’s working truth, give it a human occupant.

### Sabeth Rill

Sabeth once worked in the estate’s linen rooms. She left the district years after the Retreat and has recently returned.

She knows which doors stick, which stair is safest, and where the service wing can be entered unseen.

She sleeps in a former guest bedroom because it is dry and has a functioning shutter.

She is not keeping the room for its former owner.

She is living in it.

### Why she has not approached the Gardens

Sabeth is carrying out paid reconnaissance for a southern claimant.

She has been asked to establish what remains usable, who occupies the estate’s land, and whether the water system can be restored.

She accepted because she needed payment.

She has not yet decided what to report.

Some of the people below might remember her. That does not mean they would forgive her purpose.

### Evidence before explanation

The player can find:

- Fresh crumbs beneath an old bed.
- A recently repaired shutter fastening.
- Footprints that stop where someone changed into soft indoor shoes.
- A small wrapped writing kit.
- Notes describing occupied cottages by position rather than household name.

Those notes are more threatening than a weapon.

They are not proof that an army is coming.

### What Sabeth wants

She wants to complete enough of the commission to be paid without giving the claimant everything needed to dispossess the gardeners.

That may be impossible.

Her report could acknowledge the settlement’s improvements and strengthen an argument for continued occupation.

It could also establish the property’s renewed value.

She should not be able to resolve that contradiction with one clever sentence.

## 12. Using the house in play

Halwen House works best as several connected expeditions rather than one complete clearance.

| Purpose | What the player needs | What remains afterward |
|---|---|---|
| Investigate water loss | Notes, inspection, access to channels | Excavation and repairs |
| Recover timber | Tools, support materials, carrying capacity | Other unstable rooms |
| Search the records | Careful handling and someone able to interpret them | Disputed meaning and enforcement |
| Make salvage safer | Identify and remove a local threat | Structural and weather hazards |
| Find the hidden occupant | Follow evidence or watch the house | Sabeth’s unresolved commission |
| Establish a temporary shelter | Clear a room and assess its exits | Fuel, security, and maintenance needs |

A secured kitchen can remain useful even if the upper floor is never entered.

Recovered fittings can improve the cistern without making the estate habitable.

A conversation with Sabeth can matter even if the player refuses to help her.

## 13. What the Gardens remembers

The residents do not share one account of the house.

For Nera, it contains knowledge her community needs.

For Bren, it contains materials people can still use.

For Sella, it contains the inaccessible parts of a system she is blamed for failing to maintain.

For older residents, particular windows still belong to particular people.

They may say:

> “That was the room where she kept us waiting.”

Or:

> “He gave my mother that blanket.”

Or:

> “We were told there would be room on the next wagon.”

Halwen House becomes important because the people below it are still living with what happened there.

At dusk, someone in the Gardens notices that the upstairs shutter has closed.

No wind moved it.

They watch for a while before deciding whom to tell.
