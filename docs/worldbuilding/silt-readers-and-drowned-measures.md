## Scope and regional premise

The Silt Readers are twelve people, including dependants, sharing survey work, equipment, food obligations and shelter at the Dry Table. They began dating observations after an old crossing mark survived while its supporting bank moved. Their profession became a household, not a surviving royal bureau.

Their principle: 'A depth mark without a date is a lie.' Their necessary qualification: 'A reading is not a promise.'

The Drowned Measures are a proposed low basin reached from the Bell Road's quarry approach: submerged field boundaries, old drainage works, occupied embankments and routes that change with water and bank condition. Exact compass placement, distances, metric map, chunk placement and hydrological connection to the quarry remain unset. This is not a replacement for Reedwake or an implemented world-map change.

The region asks: who is entitled to stay dry when protecting one bank sends water toward another? Its visual identity is horizontal distance, dark water, pale posts, drowned hedges, occupied roofs and ash collected in lee corners. There is no basin-wide master lever.

## The three principals

Ulla Renn keeps the sheets. She teaches her notation and makes duplicates so the archive can survive her, but withholds current detailed sheets when she believes releasing them would destroy the household's income. Her public reference-book project might preserve the service or make its monopoly unnecessary.

Neris Kett makes field observations. She records failed soundings and uncertain references rather than cleaning them out of a report. She gives immediate danger warnings freely, but returning to check one route can consume the escort time promised elsewhere. At the quarry she investigates a possible drainage connection; she does not assume either guilt or independence without evidence.

Dain Orrel repairs poles and escorts crossings. He sees the boots, joints and meals consumed by unpaid work. He proposes small witnessed household contributions to support public notices, while detailed escorts remain paid. Residents reasonably fear another tithe; his proposal does not already have their consent.

The faction's contradiction is not false measurement but unequal timeliness: paying clients often receive the freshest detailed information while poorer people receive observations after conditions have changed. Immediate warnings remain free, which does not settle where immediate danger ends and economic ruin begins.

## How they predict the next shift

A shift means a local change in level, current, bank stability or route usability. It is not a predicted earthquake, magical relocation or guaranteed basin-wide event. Their method is repeated observation, comparison and conditional judgment. The rules below are fictional worldbuilding, not real flood-safety guidance.

### 1. Fix the reference before reading the water

Each working station has a named reference and, where possible, a second independent check. A peg alone is not enough. A stone inscription, socket or masonry course can help establish whether the peg moved. Each reference has its own history; stone can also settle.

If two references disagree, the Readers suspend comparisons across that station until they understand why. They do not average incompatible marks into a comforting number. The missing reference creates work for a survey, not permission to invent precision.

### 2. Read the same places at witnessed intervals

A field sheet records observer, place, reference, time or agreed sequence, level, current direction, bottom contact and limits. Observations at departure and return can establish a trend; extra visits are needed when conditions visibly change.

They compare an upper station, a constriction and a lower station when access permits. A rise at only one place suggests a different problem from a rise observed across several. Neither pattern alone proves its cause. An inaccessible station is written as missing, never silently carried forward.

### 3. Separate water changes from damaged ground

They note fresh silt against masonry, debris collecting at an opening, leaning posts, new cracks, disturbed repairs and whether floating scraps change direction. A sounding records where a pole touched and whether the bottom felt firm, soft or uncertain. It does not prove that the entire crossing will carry a cart.

Readers distinguish observed signs from explanations. 'New debris at west mouth' is evidence. 'Quarry water caused this' remains a hypothesis until connected observations support it.

### 4. Compare known events with earlier sequences

The archive groups previous observations by local circumstances: sustained rain reported upstream, thaw, channel clearing, bank repair, debris release or a changed gate opening. A runner's report is dated and attributed. Rumor can prompt inspection but cannot become a measured fact by being copied.

Earlier sequences suggest what to watch next, not a universal timetable. The basin has changed since many records were made. A new blockage can make the old sequence misleading.

### 5. Issue a conditional window, not an exact prophecy

Every forecast names the affected route, current observations, expected change, usable planning window, confidence basis and what would invalidate it. They never certify water as safe to drink or predict every hidden predator.

Example: 'Long Cut eastern boards. Two readings rising; lower mouth not inspected. Foot passage observed this morning, loaded passage not tested. Prefer return before the next scheduled reading. If debris begins moving against the western post, withdraw and report. Confidence limited.'

This is a planning notice, not a promise that the boards remain usable until a timer expires. The observer can recommend no crossing when evidence is inadequate.

### 6. Publish three distinct notices

- Observation: what was directly seen, where and when.
- Outlook: what may follow if stated conditions continue.
- Withdrawal: a previous recommendation no longer applies.

No mark means unknown, not safe. A newly issued sheet does not erase older evidence: superseded copies are crossed out with the reason and date. Forecasts are reassessed after their scheduled observation, relevant new reports or direct warning signs.

### 7. Carry corrections to people, not only the archive

Ulla keeps a list of clients and posted boards that received a recommendation. If it changes materially, an available runner carries a correction to reachable recipients. They cannot warn someone whose route or destination was concealed.

The correction board at the Dry Table is public. Their internal dispute is whether a crop-threatening delay deserves the same free circulation as an immediately lethal crossing. No settlement or player learns an update without an actual delivery, observation or conversation.

### Forecast failure and accountability

The Readers retain wrong forecasts with the observations behind them. After a loss, a witnessed review asks whether the references were sound, the uncertainty stated, and a correction withheld or simply impossible to deliver. An honest uncertain forecast can be wrong without being fraud. Falsified observations or concealed revisions are different offenses.

Possible remedies are refunded service, replacement work, public correction or removal from surveying duties by the household. No automatic execution, supernatural oath enforcement or all-seeing reputation system.

## Landmarks of the Drowned Measures

Proposed route web:

```text
Bell Road quarry approach
          |
     The Dry Table
          |
       Milebank -------- The Peg Houses
          |                    |
    The Three Mouths ---- The Long Cut
          |
      Lower Measures
          |
      Far Bank Gate
```

Connections are topological. Actual lengths and crossings await a metric pass. Optional sub-landmarks below do not imply extra guaranteed routes.

### The Dry Table

An old weighing house on a raised foundation. The mechanism is gone; its long stone table survives. A low roof, oilcloth-covered record cupboard and rack of banded poles distinguish it from an inn. At night the work surface must be cleared for bedding. Dry floor is never unlimited.

Outside are two boards: immediate warnings and services/debts. The second is larger. Ulla works beneath a repaired upper shutter; Dain keeps pole joints near the hearth but records away from sparks. Neris leaves wet gear in a screened entry.

Useful interactions: obtain public warnings without payment, commission a cargo-specific survey, copy an older sheet, negotiate shelter or help repair the gutter. A soaked visitor competing with open records for dry space provides conflict without a mandatory attack.

### Milebank and the Red Stitch

Milebank is a narrow raised road supporting several occupied homes. Short doors face the crest; woodpiles and rain vessels must not consume the shared passage. Old house foundations remain beneath the water outside the bank.

The Red Stitch is a visible repair seam of darker packed material held by salvaged boards. Red-brown cloth identifies the work area, not a universal danger code. Berra Tull, a bank maintainer in a shortened patched coat and brown kerchief, keeps its inspection history. She watches where visitors step before asking their names.

Her dispute: established households can substitute materials for bank labor, while newcomers are usually expected to enter the water. Repairing the seam can actually improve the bank. It does not instantly create enough dry rooms for everyone.

### The Peg Houses and the Crooked Pair

Homes occupy surviving high foundations around old survey posts. Some are stone rooms, others timber additions, connected by short local walks rather than one continuous deck. Flat scrap roofs and hanging drying screens make low irregular silhouettes.

The Crooked Pair are two posts once used together. One now leans. The local observation book and the Readers' sheet therefore appear to disagree. Neither side must be lying. Orenna Sedge, broad-handed in an oversized grey coat with shortened sleeves, wants the notation taught locally so residents can maintain their own records.

Useful interaction: establish an independent reference and a witnessed copying arrangement. Residents are not obliged to join the Silt Readers in exchange for being taught.

### The Three Mouths

Three drainage openings meet below broken masonry. One remains partly useful, one holds debris, and a third has been narrowed by successive local repairs. Pale water stains and older tool sockets show that the present arrangement was not the original one.

The accessible maintenance shelf is not the same as a safe crossing through the mouths. Large tools require a haul route. Reading the water from above is possible without entering it. Each intervention changes a particular opening, not the entire basin at once.

Useful conflict: a household's recent patch protects its occupied room while worsening a neighboring route. First reinforcing the room may permit a limited reopening that benefits both. Warn of the tradeoff before work begins.

### The Long Cut and the Four Steps

A straight drainage channel promises a simple journey but its maintenance walk survives only in sections. Reeds conceal gaps; collapsed retaining walls interrupt the banks. The Four Steps are exposed masonry on one side used as a local station, provided their stability is checked. They are not a universal zero level for the basin.

Deep pockets can shelter Reed-jaws; flooded recesses may hold Harrow eels. Greymaw wait on dry approaches rather than occupying all water. Use signs before encounters: disturbed reeds, a slide, scraps above the waterline. No mandatory predator roll on every visit.

Useful choice: observe the next broken section from higher ground, hire an escort, or take a longer bank route. Straight appearance does not establish continuous passage.

### The Lower Measures and the Drowned Orchard

A field of submerged boundary walls, occasional ridge tops and old planting lines. From a bank, a low-water interval reveals an orchard's ordered stumps. Most are not useful stepping stones. The region's former cultivation becomes visible without becoming a giant loot field.

The orchard offers a disputed fixed masonry socket that might restore an older reference. Recovering it requires comparison and documentation, not merely retrieving a glowing quest object. Changes in visibility do not establish a dependable seasonal calendar until observed repeatedly.

Useful finds: a recoverable fitting, a dated boundary inscription, a lost work tool. Heavy salvage still requires an exit route and ownership inquiry where claims exist.

### Far Bank Gate and the Return Shelf

Two gate piers rise above a drowned estate approach. The doors are gone. A covered platform, the Return Shelf, holds recovered belongings awaiting identification: a shoe, tool, strap or carrying-frame piece. It is not an unowned reward chest.

Travelers can wait for an escort, compare delivered notices and leave a message naming their next destination. At low visibility the piers are useful landmarks, but an intermittent view of them does not prove a route between viewer and gate.

People sometimes hear tapping east of the shelf. Drift against submerged stone can explain some occurrences; others remain untraced. The Readers record direction, timing and limits. This establishes neither a new monster nor an explanation of the Hush.

## Rules enforced on travelers

### Authority and limits

The Readers can set conditions for their own escorts, loaned equipment, archive rooms and maintained approaches. They do not govern the whole basin, own every crossing or overrule household rights. Joint rules at Milebank and the Peg Houses require a witnessed local agreement. Travelers may refuse a paid service and use another lawful route. The faction does not confiscate everyone’s packs or compel permanent membership.

### The crossing articles

1. State the party and load. Before a paid escort, disclose people, animals and cargo relevant to the route assessment. No magical weight detection or unrelated inventory search. A concealed load can end the escort before entry; emergency rescue is not automatically withheld.
2. Do not change a dated mark. Moving, copying or removing a working marker without authorization requires correction. A traveler who accidentally damages one must report it; a temporary conspicuous warning is better than hiding the loss. Learning and keeping private observations is not prohibited.
3. Keep the passage clear. No resting, trading or abandoned cargo on a narrow maintained walk. Use designated waiting ground. Injured people receive practical assistance rather than punishment for moving slowly.
4. Follow spacing agreed for that crossing. The escort identifies the next waiting point and who moves first. There is no universal safe spacing or load claim valid on every bank. Refusal can cancel the service before exposure; the party is not left stranded to prove a point.
5. No unannounced interference. Do not open a drainage barrier, cut a retaining tie, shift a lodged load or run machinery during an escort without warning those affected. Emergency action remains reviewable in context, not automatically criminal.
6. Respect the turnaround. The escort names the observation limit and return conditions before departure. They may stop the service at the last verified ground when conditions change. They cannot order someone into danger merely to finish a contract.
7. Keep noise proportionate. Wrap loose metal near marked listening ground; no bells or deliberate animal baiting on an escorted route without agreement. This does not imply every sound awakens the Hush or that no one may call for help.
8. Keep recovered belongings identifiable. Record where an object was found before moving it; leave a message when taking necessary equipment in an emergency. The Return Shelf is not free loot. Ownership disputes require claimants and evidence, not an omniscient theft flag.
9. Report a failed crossing or altered route. Reports may be brief and uncertain. Immediate warnings are passed on without charging the reporter. No forced interrogation of a traumatized survivor.
10. Settle the agreed price, not a surprise debt. Escort terms name payment, scope, cancellation and any equipment loan. No retroactive lifetime service or fee invented after rescue. Claims against someone unable to pay are witnessed and bounded, not enforced through food confiscation by default.

### Enforcement and restitution

Ordinary sequence: explain the breach; stop the unsafe activity; return to verified ground where possible; record a witnessed account; negotiate repair, replacement or defined service; suspend future optional contracts for serious repeated breaches. Immediate violence or deliberate sabotage can justify proportionate restraint, not unlimited collective punishment.

No one loses free immediate danger notices because of debt. Dependants are not held as security. No faction instantly learns an unwitnessed breach. A local household may dispute a Reader's account and request another witness. Corrupt enforcement is possible as a story event, not an invisible automatic rule.

The hardest case: a hungry traveler cuts a marker pole for fuel. Neris wants a replacement warning erected immediately. Dain wants the consumed pole replaced. Ulla wants a public record of what happened. Resolving the immediate danger need not settle who pays that night.

## Regional arc: The Line That Moved

Milebank and the Peg Houses accuse each other after contradictory readings. One reference shifted, explaining part of the disagreement. A blocked passage and a protective private repair explain further changes, subject to actual inspection. Quarry involvement remains an open question, not the obligatory culprit.

Players can recover a reference, compare stations, inspect repairs, escort Neris and negotiate labor. Reinforce the endangered room before a limited reopening where feasible. A shared repair, private protection, funded public survey service or deferred crossing each leaves visible persistent changes. Completed work remains useful; no hidden catastrophe invalidates every success.
