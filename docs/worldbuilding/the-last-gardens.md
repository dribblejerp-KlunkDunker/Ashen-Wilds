## Location write-up

From the Bell Road, the Last Gardens looks like six cottages behind stone walls, with green leaves showing above the nearest beds.

Closer, you see that four cottages have no doors. Their windows are packed with rubble. The garden walls have been raised with roof tiles, broken quernstones, and pieces of carved lintel.

A woman is brushing ash from cabbage leaves with a bundle of feathers. She works carefully, holding each leaf from beneath so it does not tear.

She sees you.

She finishes the plant before calling anyone.

The Last Gardens survives through **attention**: to water levels, weather, damaged leaves, missing tools, and the amount a stranger can carry beneath a coat.

Everything below is proposed lore, expanding the settlement rather than fixing its final population or gameplay statistics.

### What the place used to be

Before the Smothering, these were the south gardens of a roadside estate called Halwen House.

The cottages belonged to its gardeners, carters, and groundskeepers. Produce went uphill to the estate kitchens. Surplus went to passing travelers.

The estate family left during the Retreat.

Their servants were told to maintain the property until arrangements could be made for them.

The first winter, the gardeners took apart the glasshouses for fuel and repairs. In the second, they opened the estate seed room. By the fourth, Halwen House was uninhabitable.

The gardens lasted because people were still working them.

The settlement’s name came later.

Travelers heading north would ask where they could buy fresh food.

> “At the gardens. Last ones.”

Nobody who lives here calls it Halwen anymore.

### The settlement on the ground

The occupied buildings stand on a modest rise beside the road. The cultivated plots descend toward a shallow drainage hollow.

The layout matters because the same slope that carries water away from the houses can carry it toward the beds.

Halwen House stands farther uphill, screened by a neglected belt of trees. Its roofline is visible from the courtyard when the air is clear.

#### The waiting gates

Each road-facing garden gate has a bare rectangle of ground beside it.

Visitors put down their packs there.

They do not lean on the wall, touch the growing screens, or step among the beds. Someone comes out to speak with them.

A flat stone offers a place to sit.

It was placed there after an elderly traveler collapsed while waiting. The rule did not change. The waiting became slightly less cruel.

#### The two inhabited houses

The larger cottage contains the shared sleeping rooms. Its former partitions have been removed to make room for raised sleeping platforms.

The smaller contains the kitchen, water-boiling hearth, and a workroom.

Neither has enough space for everyone to live comfortably.

Private arguments occur within hearing of other households. Couples hang blankets. Children learn to pretend they have not heard.

#### The ruined cottages

The four empty cottages remain useful.

One stores fuel. One holds broken equipment awaiting repair. Another is a quarantine shelter with a leaking roof.

The fourth is kept empty because its rear wall is moving.

People periodically suggest dismantling it.

The household that once lived there still occupies a corner of the sleeping house. They object every time.

### What they actually eat

The gardens do not produce enough to support the settlement alone.

Roots and cabbage form the dependable part of their harvest. Flax provides some repair material and trade goods. Better-protected beds hold onions and whatever seed the households can obtain.

They still need grain, fat, salt, and protein from elsewhere.

That makes the visible greenery misleading.

A traveler may look over the wall and see abundance. The residents see weeks of work that can be eaten once.

The smallest leaves go into soup. Damaged roots are cut and used first. Seed plants stand in a separate enclosure that nobody harvests without permission.

Children know the seed enclosure is not a place to look for food.

Some have done it anyway.

### Three people who keep it functioning

#### Sella Morn, keeper of the water

Sella maintains the cistern, allocates water for the beds, and oversees boiling for sale.

She is not an elected leader. She knows the system better than anyone else, and the settlement has gradually allowed that knowledge to become authority.

She carries a narrow measuring rod stained at several heights.

Every morning, she checks the cistern before speaking to customers.

When the level falls unexpectedly, she stops selling water. This costs the settlement income and makes travelers angry. It also makes people inside the gardens accuse her of protecting her own beds first.

Her private failing is that she sometimes checks the level alone and reports it afterward.

She has not falsified it.

She dislikes being asked to prove that.

> “You may look. Keep your hands off the rope.”

#### Bren Tal, strap-maker and gate attendant

Bren repairs harness, pack straps, boots, and the leather parts of handcarts.

His workbench stands close enough to the road that he can inspect arrivals without leaving a repair unfinished.

He is patient with frightened people and suspicious of confident ones.

Bren introduced the rule that stored bundles must be opened. He also maintains the storage tally and remembers which travelers return when promised.

He quietly extends credit to a few customers.

The other households do not know how much food and material this represents.

If all those customers failed to return, Bren could not replace what he has advanced.

#### Nera Halwen, keeper of seed

Nera’s surname is a problem.

Her grandmother was a servant at the estate. Her father used the estate name when the old household records became irrelevant.

A visiting claimant has since suggested this makes Nera part of the former proprietor’s household—and therefore evidence that the estate has remained continuously occupied.

Nera considers the claim nonsense.

She also possesses the best remaining knowledge of the old gardens: which plots were raised, which drains connect, and what grew beneath the demolished glasshouses.

She keeps seed in labeled containers inside a locked room.

She will trade food more readily than seed.

> “You’re hungry now. So are we. That isn’t the only year there is.”

### How decisions are made

The households meet in the kitchen after the evening pot has been served.

They settle ordinary matters by agreement:

- Which beds receive the next water.
- Who takes the roadside watch.
- What repairs can wait.
- Whether a traveler’s offer is worth accepting.
- How much food can be sold rather than eaten.

Agreement becomes harder when a decision affects sleeping space.

The settlement recognizes households, not individuals. A person admitted into an existing household gains food and floor space through it.

Someone without a sponsoring household may work outside the walls for days without acquiring a place inside.

This arrangement protects the residents from unlimited demands.

It also lets an established household make an outsider dependent on its goodwill.

Bren has twice argued for a common guest place that belongs to no household.

Nobody has volunteered to surrender the sleeping space it would require.

### The cistern beneath the courtyard

The cistern is older than the cottages.

Rain from the surviving roofs passes through maintained channels into a covered chamber below the paved courtyard. A separate overflow runs toward the lower hollow.

The chamber has an inspection opening, but reaching its far end requires removing paving and lifting a heavy cover stone.

Part of the shared sleeping house stands over the access route.

The recent loss of water has divided the settlement.

Sella suspects a damaged lining.

Nera suspects the old estate channels were never fully closed.

Several households suspect theft.

All three explanations are plausible enough to influence behavior. None is established merely because someone believes it.

#### Signs the player can investigate

- The water falls faster after certain nights, but the record is incomplete.
- Soil near one ruined cottage stays wet longer than neighboring ground.
- An old plan shows a channel extending beyond the courtyard, but does not show whether it was finished.
- A child has found a trickle emerging behind a wall uphill.
- One household has been washing cloth secretly, using more water than it admits.

The concealed washing explains some consumption. It need not explain the whole loss.

Discovering a liar should not automatically solve the infrastructure problem.

### The road trade

The Last Gardens sells small things with immediate value.

| What they offer | What limits it |
|---|---|
| Boiled water | Fuel, labor, and the cistern level |
| Hot broth | The common pot must feed residents first |
| Strap and harness repairs | Usable leather and fittings |
| Clean cloth | Water, drying space, and material |
| Short-term storage | Limited room and distrust of unknown contents |
| Fresh vegetables | Small harvests and the need to preserve seed plants |

Customers can pay in goods or useful work.

“Useful” is defined by the residents.

A skilled mason may receive a meal for an hour’s work. A hungry stranger without a trade may spend the afternoon clearing a ditch for the same portion.

The residents call this reasonable.

The stranger may call it something else.

#### The storage shed

Bren opens every bundle in front of its owner.

Goods are placed on raised shelves with a tally attached. The matching piece goes to the customer.

The dead dog found inside a grain sack remains part of settlement argument. Bren says inspection prevented worse trouble. Others think the incident became an excuse to search everything people own.

The sack’s owner never returned.

No one has established his purpose.

### Halwen House

The estate above the gardens should be a substantial local location, not merely a distant silhouette.

Its useful ground-floor rooms have been stripped repeatedly. Window frames are gone. The kitchen range has been broken for iron.

The upper floor remains dangerous.

Several things still make it worth entering:

- A blocked service passage may connect to the old water system.
- The estate’s garden plans were kept in a narrow room beside the kitchen.
- Heavy shelving could provide seasoned repair timber if dismantled safely.
- The glasshouse foundations retain buried fittings and fragments of piping.

The residents know about these possibilities.

They have not ignored free salvation. They lack the spare labor, equipment, and confidence to explore a failing building while maintaining their food supply.

#### The room nobody claims

One upstairs bedroom remains furnished.

Someone has cleared ash from the sill and folded the bedding.

There are mundane possibilities: a hidden occupant, a resident slipping away for privacy, or a traveler using the house without approaching the gardens.

The first clue should be an object out of place, not a supernatural announcement.

A fresh heel of bread on a table would be enough.

### A local custom: leaving one bed untouched

At the start of each planting, the gardeners leave a narrow bed empty until the others have been sown.

Older residents say it is for anyone who has not yet come home.

Younger people call it a reserve in case another bed fails.

Nera permits both explanations.

When seed is short, the empty bed becomes contentious.

Someone always asks how long the absent are allowed to keep taking space.

### The first conflict: water for the road

A cold spell brings an unusual number of travelers south.

Darnel Cross has trouble at its well. People arrive carrying empty containers, some with animals, some with children.

At the same time, Sella finds the cistern lower than expected.

She suspends sales.

The gardens still have enough water to distribute some, but doing so would consume part of their reserve before they know why it is falling.

Bren wants limited portions.

Nera wants the leak found first.

People begin waiting beside the gates.

This conflict gives the player several concrete interventions:

- Inspect and repair part of the water system.
- Bring containers or fuel that make controlled distribution easier.
- Help arrange temporary shelter without admitting everyone to the sleeping rooms.
- Expose concealed household consumption without pretending that settles the leak.
- Negotiate supplies from elsewhere at a cost the settlement must actually meet.

Success should leave something visible: a repaired channel, a covered waiting shelter, an agreed water measure, or a guest platform built from recovered timber.

It should not make the Last Gardens generous forever.

### The place after dark

After the evening meal, the screens are checked and the gates fastened.

Bren brings his tools inside. Sella measures the water once more. Nera counts returned seed containers.

People sleeping in the road shelter can see a little light beneath the kitchen door.

They can hear the washing-up.

Sometimes someone comes out with an extra bowl.

Sometimes nobody does.

Beyond the last wall, the abandoned garden plots continue into darkness, their paths still straight beneath the ash.
