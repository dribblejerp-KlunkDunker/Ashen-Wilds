# The Keeping and the Merewick Register

## Provenance and scope

Compiled 2026-11-01 from the worldbuilding conversation dated 2026-10-28 through 2026-11-01. This is a structured consolidation of the lore and scene beats, not a verbatim transcript or an archive of every original sentence. New theology, institutions, events, and characters remain proposed lore. Committing this document preserves them; it does not automatically settle canon. No implementation or game testing is claimed. - The collector is unnamed and his employer and jurisdiction are unspecified. Do not identify him with Collector Brannick or the Tithe without an explicit decision.
- New religious proposals do not supersede Reedwake's local moorings and return-bread customs or prove supernatural rules.

## 1. Religion throughout the Blackfen

The Keeping was the dominant regional human faith before the Smothering. Its central teaching: the world is held apart from the dark by obligations kept. Roofs shelter, hearths warm, gates are watched, dead people are named, and oaths bind those who make them. The distant god is usually called the Keeper. Local saints include drowned ferrymen, beacon tenders, and soldiers who held gateways. Divine existence, intentions, and interventions remain uncertain.

Lords owed protection; subjects owed service. Priests witnessed the reciprocal covenant but often enforced only the subjects' obligations. Religion inhabits shelter, food, burial, property, and belonging rather than a separate good-versus-evil conflict.

Proposed customs: touch a lintel when entering shelter; preserve the last hearth coal overnight; speak a corpse's name into its mouth; swear against stone rather than wood; leave bent nails at roadside shrines in hope of an open door. Customs may mix scripture, older beliefs, and emergency inventions.

### Fractures after the Smothering

- Southern hierarchy: lawful authority and observance supposedly preserve the covenant. Its clergy recognize marriages, inheritances, property, and debts, including claims against abandoned villages. Some individual priests sabotage unjust collections.
- Local faithful: a superior who deserts dependents loses the sacred basis of his authority. This justifies refusing taxes, occupying estates, or rejecting returning claimants. Saying that a lord was their lord only while he kept them expresses the doctrine.
- Penitents: survivors were left to repay a debt, not spared. They retrieve bodies, maintain warnings, and care for the sick, but may also treat suffering as payment and deny rescued people freedom to leave.

The Tithe can appropriate an existing kiln-maintenance obligation rather than invent a cult. A resident priest could bless assessments and certify seizures, sincerely believing the kiln prevents starvation. Proposed incident: burial property is exempt, so a Door-Man asks whether anyone will swear a threatened cooking pot belongs to the dead; lying risks punishment. This is proposed local abuse, not established Tithe law throughout the region.

Older marsh practices survive beneath adopted saints: offerings at pools, untouched stones, and contradictory rules about bells, silence, and fish. Some have practical effects, some accomplish nothing, and apparent supernatural results remain uncertain.

Proposed movements: the Last Hearth shelters everyone but forbids winter departure because labor sustains its fire; the Unwriters (renamed from "the Unnamed" on 2026-11-05: the game's Unnamed are the risen dead; see `CANON.md`) erase names and graves believing obligations let the catastrophe find them; scattered listeners beneath the hills suppress speech and bells but do not authoritatively explain the Vaults or what the orks fled.

Faith crosses borders through southern appointments, refugee household saints, foreign contracts, battlefield devotions, pilgrims, rival relic shrines, and conflicting burial customs. At Wayfarer's Watchhouse, keeping a hearth raises disputes about admitting infected travelers, sanctuary for debtors, murderers seeking shelter, and allocating beds. A priest might offer consecration while registering the property. A widow might consider it sacred simply because someone stayed.

## 2. How the Keeping broke

1. Senior priests blessed the slag-iron expeditions as stewardship. Worker covenants promised protection and support for dependents. Local objections to disturbing the depths were dismissed as superstition. Religious authorities helped legitimize the descent.
2. Warning bells belonged to both civic and religious infrastructure. Ringing meant stopping work, opening stores, and admitting danger. Some priests rang against orders; elsewhere ropes were cut. Obedience to superiors conflicted with warning dependents.
3. Fortified gates imposed changing refuge conditions: residence, useful trade, provisions, and current registration. Families carrying witnessed promises were refused by the institutions that had issued them. Capacity limits were real and also became cover for favoritism.
4. Evacuation proceeded through children, treasuries, archives, relics, senior clergy, and escorts. Relief was promised. Grain left under guard; villages received receipts. Some officials believed authority was being preserved to organize aid, others knew they would not return.
5. Those who honored hospitality sometimes died in crowded, contaminated shelters while closed households survived. This did not prove divine approval of cruelty, but contradicted confidence that faithful behavior held the world together.
6. Burial overwhelmed available fuel, ground, labor, and knowledge. Abbreviated rites and common graves became necessity in some settlements and sacrilege in others. Communities accused one another to defend their treatment of their own dead.
7. The surviving hierarchy spread guilt across everyone instead of answering proportionately for institutional betrayal. A stolen loaf and an emptied granary could both be called covenant-breaking; only one perpetrator retained collection power. Renewed obedience was demanded before accountability.

The Keeping survived without agreement about who remained bound to whom after a superior fled. Faith fractured rather than simply disappearing.

## 3. What survivors did after the priests fled

Departures were disguised as journeys to obtain relief. Servants kept lamps burning and repeated promises until empty traveling chests and missing winter clothes exposed the flight. Survivors opened stores collectively, sometimes with careful accounting and sometimes under armed capture. Control of food immediately required decisions about refugees, concealed stores, parish membership, and seed grain.

Gravediggers, midwives, attendants, and other useful people performed temporary rites that became permanent offices. Some congregations elected keepers and required release before departure, protecting against desertion while potentially trapping exhausted servants.

Registers became contested: widows needed service promises, tenants wanted assessments destroyed, heirs needed kinship records, fugitives wanted erasure, and strongmen wanted collectible claims. Burnings could free debtors while destroying protections for women, orphans, and heirs.

Necessary burial practices became local law. A later cleric could invalidate rites performed during his institution's absence. Altar cloth became bandages, vestments bedding, shrine timber fuel, and fittings repairs. People recorded intended repayments or left substitute offerings. Some remaining officials claimed emergency authority indefinitely.

Proposed returning-priest scene: an ordained appointee challenges a former cleaner conducting burials after years of maintaining the chapel and burying her children. She asks where he wants the bodies moved. His escort starts counting grain. The settlement divides over recognized authority versus work done in its absence.

## 4. The fate of registers beyond Merewick

Selected books and ownership charters traveled south. Losses were sometimes accidental, but enforceable debts could survive while promised protections vanished. Remaining books might be guarded by several keyholders or monopolized by an armed household charging for access.

Selective destruction separated rights from debts; indiscriminate destruction could privilege people strong enough to ignore unwritten claims. Families carried fragments sewn into clothing. Missing continuations could conceal discharge, conditions, or exemption. New community books attempted reciprocal obligations but remained vulnerable to emergency exceptions.

The Tithe might claim old obligations transferred to whoever maintained a service, while a receipt or exemption could obstruct a particular assessment without destroying the entire institution. Burial fragments might identify strangers through clothing and possessions. Proposed entry: an unnamed woman beside a sluice, red thread at her cuff, buried with a smaller child. Another page ends, after numerous deaths, with the statement that there were more.

Proposed Watchhouse-area artifact: a flood-damaged Causeway parish register lists households as admitted to shelter, later crossed out as departed. An older sheet records those same households refused entry. This establishes revision of an official account, not every perpetrator's identity.

## 5. Merewick: the division at the chapel

On the ninth day without a priest, villagers ate the chapel hens. On the tenth, they entered his room: hardened cheese, an empty traveling chest, and a pale outline where a silver Keeper had hung. The register lay wrapped beneath the desk. Mara Venn, the chapel cleaner for nineteen years in this proposed scene, carried it to the altar.

Harl demanded the debts be read: three measures of rye, two cart-service days, one unpaid levy. Soldiers had taken his cart north with his son; neither returned. No discharge had been recorded. The miller opposed arbitrary erasure until Harl demanded his entry be read.

The book mixed assessments, namings, marriages, burials, and land terms because parchment had been scarce. Harl's debt shared a leaf with his daughter's naming and the miller's water grant. Widow Senn proposed cutting. Sewing shears replaced a tearing knife. Three people read each piece: one spoke, one followed, one explained what would be separated.

A dead household's assessment was burned; its reed-cutting right was saved for the orphan Eda, whom Senn had taken in. Births went into a flour sack, burials into the gravedigger's tool chest, land and boundary pieces under candlesticks, service promises into a smaller pile. Marriages proved inseparable from children and property. A missing husband's wife received her entry to resist his brother's claim to her house.

An assessment said it was subject to remission below. The continuation had already been cut apart. A search found it beneath the turnip bowl: chapel-roof timber had discharged the debt. Afterward they searched for continuations before burning, but still missed some.

The miller's inherited diversion grant required bridge maintenance and free downstream water access. He received both right and duties together. Disputes remained: brothers would not trust each other with an inheritance; a possible prior marriage blocked another claim; fragments lost context. Mara retained shared records, households took individual papers, and the gravedigger kept burials. Six people marked the arrangement and two refused. Harl proposed a copy, but nobody had another book.

## 6. What happened to the scraps

- Harl's wife lost the daughter's naming strip overnight amid bedding, food, and hearth chores. The child knew her own name without the proof.
- Senn stored Eda's reed grant in curtain cloth inside a crock and taught her the boundary from a missing post to a bent willow. Senn died in the second winter. Eda recovered the curling original and obtained Mara's copy but kept both against the possibility of disputed transcription.
- Neighbors supplied bridge timber and confronted the miller with his grant. He helped repair the crossing and fed workers thin porridge. When he later withheld the grant, the conditions were scratched onto a public plank despite his objections.
- The missing husband's wife preserved her marriage entry in his glove. His brother challenged it because witness names lay on a separate missing fragment. She asked Harl to sleep beside her door.
- Burial scraps deteriorated and lost ordering. The gravedigger grouped wrappers by place and time. A woman identified a boy named Teren from a knife with three notches; Mara was fetched to amend the entry.
- Mara stitched blank margins, notices, and damaged merchant sheets between boards. Owners sometimes held originals while she copied. She refused a suspicious chapel-labor exemption and was accused of appointing herself priest. Copies recorded presenters and witnesses.

## 7. The collector and the witnesses

Years later, an unnamed collector arrived with certified southern copies of old assessments. He recognized Eda's grant and began considering yield. Villagers produced fragments recording promises as well as debts. He asked for witnesses.

He marked the miller with a small hooked stroke after learning the miller benefited from the grant. He asked Harl's widow for a living direct witness or receipt for the cart requisition. None survived. Initially he recorded her admission of destroying an assessment; Mara insisted the cart claim also be written and read aloud.

The collector distinguished authentic documents from what they legally established: marriage did not automatically settle house ownership, timber might be an already-required service, and guardianship needed evidence. The gravedigger identified Eda's parents and their burial, supporting recognition of her reed-cutting right but not immunity from assessment.

The miller presented his right without conditions; Mara produced the separate duty fragment. Three witnesses, including his son, described the original passage and bridge work. After separate questioning, the collector recognized the grant with obligations. The miller could not retract the already-presented evidence merely because the conditions inconvenienced him.

Villagers scrutinized every line thereafter. Harl's cart assessment was deferred pending review, not discharged, and the deferral was written. They bought copying paper with salt. Mara copied, the miller checked, Eda dried pages, and the widow heard her deferral twice. The collector left with revised schedules and unresolved disputes. Two households surrendered grain and one promised labor. No general forgiveness occurred. The local copy ended with a heading for witnesses.

## 8. The miller's mark

Mara had copied the hook exactly because she did not know its meaning. The miller learned from a market copyist that it usually indicated an interested witness, depending on the clerk, not necessarily a liar. Rumor nevertheless converted interest into rejected testimony and then dishonesty. Customers watched his measures. He began publicly leveling and announcing every portion.

Someone scratched the hook into his door and shutter. He shaved it off, leaving pale thin wood. He wrongly seized a boy; the boy's mother threatened to withdraw help clearing the mill wheel. His own son later admitted carving the shutter because his father kept denying what he had tried to withhold. They repaired another shifted bridge support together.

At the next circuit, the miller demanded an explanation beside the mark. The collector confirmed interested witness, not false testimony, and that the grant had been recognized subject to obligations. Mara copied the clarification and it was read publicly. Neighbors did not excuse the attempted omission, but some stopped watching every measure. The measure remained openly available.

In an undated later vignette, the elderly miller's son explains a thin shutter patch to a new boy. Rather than summarize the mark misleadingly, he takes the child to the chapel to read the book. The mark persists with context rather than being magically erased.

## Design principles carried forward

- Documents require interpretation, jurisdiction, witnesses, and enforcement; a stolen page does not automatically dissolve an institution.
- Honest records can preserve both protections and extraction. Missing records can harm the powerless.
- Local victories remain real: a bridge works, a right is recognized, and a collection is deferred, without healing the region.
- Accusations and consequences require discoverable information; no collector has supernatural knowledge.
- Religious claims are not verified cosmology or disease mechanics.
- Resolve the Mara Venn and Harl name conflicts and the scene dates before canon integration.
