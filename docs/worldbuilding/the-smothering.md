# The Smothering: ash, spores and the six weathers

**Status: PROPOSED** (Godot addition, 2026-11-05). Written for this repository under `CANON.md`, whose
facts and tone rules bind it. Nothing here is canon until the owner says so. Where this file gives
a number for a weather, a spore cloud or a husk, the number is the shipped Godot build's
(`scripts/world/weather.gd`, `scripts/world/spore_cloud.gd`, `scripts/enemies/enemy_spore_husk.gd`);
if the code changes, the code wins and this file is corrected.

**Summary.** Thirty winters after the Breaking, the Smothering is not an event but the weather
people live in. This file describes it from the inside: what the ash tastes like and what it does
to lungs, wells, roofs, wool and iron; the short grey days, the cold and the few weeks of mud
called spring; the argument over whether it is thinning. It sets out what survivors know and guess
about the ash-spores, how ash-lung, corpse-caps and the walking blooms fit together (fact 18), and
why fire, wind and sleet do what they do to a spore cloud. It walks through the six weathers as the
Godot build plays them, with folk names, sayings and the dangers of each; then fire and fuel, the
hearth and the custom of the last coal; then food, salt, water and hunger. It ends with sayings and
weather banners in plain ASCII, ready for the HUD.

The archive's habit holds throughout: say what happened, say what you do not know. Guesses are
marked as guesses. Designer ideas are marked **Hook:**.

**Owner decision O1 (2026-11-05, in `CANON.md`):** only humans are
people. Goblins, orks, ogres, trolls and every other creature are animals or monsters, with no
language, names, culture, faith, bargains or society. The archive was revised to match the same day; the orks' fear of the Dead Calm is written as animal
behaviour.

## 1. The ash as lived

### How it falls

The ash comes on the wind, mostly from the north. On an ordinary night it falls as soft grey
flakes, the size of a fingernail, slow and a little sideways, like burnt paper drifting down from
a bonfire nobody can see. A flake looks soft and is not: rub it between finger and thumb and it
breaks into grit, fine as flour and sharp as sand. Under the flakes, never seen, is a finer dust
that hangs in every room, settles on every shelf and is in every mouthful.

It does not fall evenly. It drifts against walls and fills the lee of a hedge. Open ground is
scoured to the mud by one wind and buried ankle deep by the next. A road walked every day stays
dark with feet; a road abandoned for a month turns the same grey as the fields beside it, which
is how the Blackfen loses its paths.

When the wind stops, the ash stops. Every child in the fen knows this before they can say why,
and it is the first evidence anyone gives that the ash is carried, not made in the sky. It is also
why the still weathers are frightening: no wind, no ash, no cover.

### Taste and smell

Dry ash tastes of a cold hearth and struck flint, bitter at the back of the tongue, with an iron
edge that people say is stronger before a squall. Everything tastes of it a little: bread, water,
the inside of your own mouth on waking. Children raised in it do not notice; people who remember
the years before say they have not tasted clean water since the first winter.

Wet ash smells sour and sharp, like lye left standing in a bucket. A house with a leaking roof
smells of it all winter. Dry ash has almost no smell of its own, which is why the sweet smell of
mushrooms on the air stops conversation: that is not ash, that is spores (section 2).

### Lungs and eyes

Everyone coughs. A morning cough that brings up grey is so common that the Watchhouse does not
count it as sickness, and this is the root of every argument about admitting travellers: the grey
cough of ordinary ash and the grey cough that is the first stage of ash-lung sound the same
(`bestiary.md`, *Ash-lung*). Healers disagree about whether ash alone can carry someone to the
crust. The book has not settled it, and neither does this file.

The ordinary remedies are cloth and water. People wear a fold of wet linen over mouth and nose
outdoors, wrung out at every stop. A dry mask is worse than none, because it fills and is breathed
through. In the worst falls, people breathe through wet wool and walk with their eyes half shut.

Ash scratches eyes. Red rims and a gritty, streaming squint are the Blackfen face; carriers call
it **ash-eye**. Eyes are washed with boiled water from the palm, never rubbed. An eye rubbed raw
in the field can cloud for good, and the book counts more one-eyed carters than it counts
sword-wounds.

### Crops and beasts

The ash did not kill the harvest by itself. The cold did: summer did not come back (fact 5), and
grain needs a summer. What the ash did was finish what the cold began. It lies on leaves and
starves them of what little light there is, and a wet fall sticks to a bed of greens like plaster.
Gardeners wash leaves with a hand-brush and a pot of water, leaf by leaf, which is why a fresh
cabbage costs what it costs.

Grazing failed first. Sheep and cattle that ate ash with every mouthful wore their teeth to the
gum in two winters and walked as if their joints were full of gravel. Fen folk call it
**ash-tooth**, and an old ewe that still has her teeth is shown to visitors. Most flocks were
eaten, abandoned or went wild in the first years (`bestiary.md`, *Three causes*).

### Wells and water

Ash in water turns it grey and bitter and puts grit in the teeth. Open wells were lidded in the
first winter; the ones that were not are still known by name and avoided. Rain-butts are skimmed
every morning. Water drawn from a mere or a drain is left standing overnight in a crock so the ash
sinks, the clear part is poured off through cloth, and then it is boiled (section 5). Darnel
Cross's trouble at its well, and the Last Gardens' falling cistern, are what water means in the
Smothering: not thirst exactly, but the fuel, the labour and the trust it takes to make water safe.

### Roofs

Dry ash is light. Wet ash is as heavy as clay, and it does not run off. A roof that held a winter
of snow in the old years will come down under a fall of ash followed by sleet, and in the first
thaw whole streets did. Thatch rots under a crust of wet ash in a season; slate and stone hold
best, which is one more reason people moved into chapels, toll houses and the Watchhouse rather
than their own cottages.

Sweeping the roof is the first and most hated chore of every grey morning, done from the ridge by
the lightest person in the household, on a rope. Falls from roofs kill more people in the
Watchhouse's record than goblins do.

### Wool and cloth

Ash works into wool and grinds it from inside, so cloth wears through at the elbow, the knee and
under the pack strap. Everything is patched and everything is grey, whatever its first colour;
clean water is too dear to waste on shirts. The ash-moth's grubs eat stored wool (`bestiary.md`),
so winter cloth is hung in smoke, not folded in a chest.

### Iron

Wet ash eats iron. A blade left damp in its sheath overnight comes out orange along the edge; a
hinge pin left a winter seizes solid. The Rustlings take what the ash softens (`bestiary.md`).
Every scrap of tallow that is not eaten goes on iron, and an ungreased tool is a careless person's
tool. The Rusted Mail Hauberk in the ork plunder pile ("orange at every ring") is what thirty
winters do to mail nobody greased.

*Guess:* slag-iron is said to rust slower than common iron. Smiths at the Nailhouse swear to it;
nobody has tested it fairly, and everyone who owns slag-iron has a reason to say so.

### The short grey days

The sun, when it shows at all, is a pale coin behind the ash, a disc you can look at directly. A
winter noon is a long dusk; the useful part of a day is a few hours either side of it. Shadows are
soft or absent, and people who were children in S.0 say the thing they miss is not warmth but
edges.

The difference between day and night has worn thin, and most of what the Watchhouse does it does
in the dark, because in winter the dark is most of what there is. Nights are black under ash and
cold-blue when the moon cuts through it. A clear moon is a mixed gift (see Hoarfrost).

### The cold

The cold is the Smothering's real weapon. Frost comes in every month. Snow has fallen in what
should have been harvest. The fen rarely freezes solid, because it is too wet and too deep, but
the meres skin over at the edges and the ground rings under the boot. People sleep clothed, in
heaps, beside the fire, and the question of who sleeps nearest it is the question of who the
household values most (section 4). Orks feel the cold badly (`bestiary.md`): they den under
stone, and a pack will fight anything for a dry roof.

### Spring: the thaw and the floods

Spring still comes, barely (fact 9): a few grey weeks when the ground softens, the frost lets go
and the ash of the whole winter turns to mud at once. People call it spring because there is no
other word for it. Carriers call it **the grey mud**: knee deep on the verges, deeper in the
lanes, and slick enough to take a cart off a bank.

Then the fen floods. Meltwater from the uplands comes down every drain and river at once, the
meres join hands across the low fields, and the Blackfen becomes a sheet of grey water with roofs
and dead trees standing out of it. The Old Causeway exists for these weeks; for most of the thaw
it is the only dry line across the wet, which is why whoever holds its choke point holds the fen.

The thaw is also when the dead come up. Floodwater lifts shallow graves and turns out the common
pits, and corpse-caps bloom along every new tideline within days. Lime is never dearer than in
spring. The Watchhouse counts its winter dead in spring, too, because that is when the bodies are
found.

And the thaw is the planting (section 5). Everyone tries to last until spring *(in game)*. What
they mean is: until the first mud, when the eels run and there is something to put in the ground.

### Is it thinning?

Some say the ash is thinning. No one can prove it (fact 9). The Watchhouse argues about it every
winter with the same evidence on both sides.

**For thinning:**

- The oldest residents say the days are a shade lighter than they were in the first ten years, and
  that the sun throws a shadow at noon now, on some days, which it did not then.
- The Watchhouse's **ash-post**, a doorpost cut each spring with a notch at the height of the
  deepest drift against it, shows shallower notches in the recent winters than in the early ones.
- Birch seedlings have come up on the Causeway verges, where nothing came up for twenty years.
- The Last Gardens grows onions under cover, and they say they could not once.

**Against:**

- The ash-post was cut by many hands, one year is missing, and the post was moved when the north
  wall was rebuilt. Some of the "early" notches may be from a bad drift, not a bad winter.
- Carriers from the Bell Road say the hills to the north still smoke, and the ash on the high road
  is as deep as ever.
- The worst winter some residents remember is a recent one, not an early one.
- Quarry folk argue that what falls now is old ash lifted off the uplands by the wind, not new ash
  at all. If so, a "thinner" fall says nothing about the source, only about the wind.
- Everyone wants it to be thinning. *The ash is thinning, said every grandmother since the second
  winter.*

*What no one knows:* whether the vents are still venting, whether the cold would break if the ash
stopped, and whether the old are remembering the sky or remembering being young.

**Hook:** the ash-post as a readable prop in the Watchhouse: thirty notches, a gap, two hands of
handwriting, and a dispute among residents about what it shows. A later act could let the player
add this winter's notch.

## 2. The ash-spores

The spores came up from the Vaults with everything else (fact 6). They are not the ash, though
they travel with it and the word "ash-spore" makes them sound like one thing.

### What survivors know

- **They are a dust.** Finer than ash, pale grey-green, and sweet-smelling: the smell of mushrooms
  where there should be rot (`bestiary.md`, *Corpse-caps*). That smell is the only warning most
  people get.
- **They fruit in the dead.** Corpse-caps rise from the unburied dead in damp ground within days
  and puff spores when brushed. Lime and fire stop the bloom (fact 16).
- **A few of the sick bloom before they finish dying** (fact 18): the caps root while the body is
  still dying, and it walks with them. These are the **walking blooms**, or husks.
- **Breathing a cloud chokes you.** The chest locks, the eyes stream, breath will not come back,
  and you can hardly see. It passes once you are out of the cloud.
- **A cloud alone rarely kills.** Nobody in the warning book died of a spore cloud by itself. They
  died of what came for them while they were on their knees coughing.
- **Fire burns them. Still air keeps a cloud; wind and sleet clear it** (fact 18; below).

### Ash-lung, corpse-caps and the walking blooms

The book's open question stands: whether breathing the caps' puff begins ash-lung, or whether the
sick only bloom because they were already dying of it (`bestiary.md`). This file does not answer
it. What the Watchhouse can say is how the three things sit together in what people see:

| What people see | When | What it means to them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The grey cough, the crust, silence | The living sick | Ash-lung, stages 1 to 3; "Ashbound" is the verdict at 3 |
| Corpse-caps on a body | Days after death | Stage 4, the bloom; lime it or burn it |
| Caps on a body that still walks | Before death has finished | A walking bloom: stage 3 and 4 at once |

A walking bloom is recognisable at a distance by the caps along its shoulders and scalp, its slow,
swaying shamble, and a crust so thick over the eyes that it barely sees. Close, the smell is sweet
enough to sicken.

Carriers who have fought them say three things, and they match the Godot husk exactly: every cut
puffs a small cloud from the wound; when it dies, the whole crop bursts at once into a cloud twice
as wide as a person is tall; and its clawing hands drive the dust straight into your face.

### What survivors guess

*Each of these is a guess. The book will not choose.*

- **The healers' guess:** the spores are the seed of the caps, and ash-lung is the spores taking
  root in a living chest. The crust is the first growth. A walking bloom is someone in whom the
  growth outran the dying.
- **The ash guess:** ash-lung is the ash and the damp, and the spores only take the already dying,
  the way flies only take meat that has turned. A walking bloom is a body that was dead in every
  way that matters before the caps came.
- **The cut-flower guess:** the bloom feeds the body it grows in and keeps it moving for a while,
  the way a cut flower in water keeps its colour. Gravediggers say this; healers dislike it.
- Ash-moths carry spores. Fen healers blame them; nobody has proven it (`bestiary.md`).
- Boar that root up the dead carry the bloom into those who eat them. Open (`bestiary.md`).
- Spores washed down by sleet lie in the mud as grey paste and wait for a body. *Guess, from
  quarry folk who say caps come up thickest where the sleet runs off.*

### Why fire burns spores

The plain cause, as any miller would tell you: the spores are a dry dust as fine as flour, and fine
dust burns. Millers in the old days feared flour hanging in a closed mill near a lamp. A spore
cloud is the same thing in the open air: a torch held into it makes it glitter and crackle, and the
cloud burns away from the flame inward. It does not flash or explode, because it is too thin and
too damp from the breath of the fen, and nobody has been burned doing it, but it goes, and fast.

Lime works on caps for a plainer reason still: quicklime dries and burns flesh, and the caps need
damp. The book does not know whether lime does anything to a cloud already in the air.

### Why still air keeps a cloud, and wind and sleet clear it

Dust hangs in still air. Anyone who has seen a sunbeam in a barn has seen it: the motes go nowhere.
A spore cloud in a windless hollow sits where it burst, swelling a little and then slowly thinning,
for as long as nothing moves it. Fog is still air that is also wet, and a cloud in it hangs longer
still.

Wind spreads a cloud thin until it is too thin to choke anyone, and a squall tears one apart in
moments. Sleet and rain wet the dust and beat it down out of the air onto the ground. In both
cases the spores have not gone anywhere: they have only gone somewhere you are not breathing.

### Goblins, orks and the cloud

*Open.* In the Outskirts people have seen goblins and orks run through a husk's cloud without
breaking stride, and the husks breathe their own clouds without coughing. Yet the far-gone sick the
Watchhouse finds in the reeds include goblins and orks as well as people (fact 17), so the spores
do take them in the end. Whether these beasts simply do not choke the way people do, or choke and
do not care, the book does not know. Some at the Watchhouse say the Vault-born were "used to it
below"; others say nobody has watched closely enough to tell.

### For the game

The Godot numbers, for whoever writes text against them:

- **A husk:** 55 health, shambles at 1.9 m/s (slower than you walk), sees 8 m in a 120 degree cone,
  hears a sprint or a swing within 8 m (scaled by your noise), claws for 16 and two seconds of
  choking.
- **Its wound puff:** a 1.2 m radius, about 3 seconds. **Its death burst:** 2.4 m, about 8
  seconds. Both lives are scaled once, when the cloud forms, by the weather's spore factor.
- **Choking:** 3 health a second while inside a cloud, never below 1; stamina returns at a quarter
  of its rate; sight drops to about a third. The HUD flags `[CHOKING: SPORES]`.
- **Fire:** a lit torch within 2 m of a cloud's edge burns it ten times faster, about a second.
- **Where:** three husks in the Spore Hollow on the yard's west edge, around the Spore-Choked Cart
  with a full torch in it.

**Hook:** the Spore-Choked Cart is someone else's unfinished job. A torch was brought here to burn
the hollow and is still in the cart. Who brought it, and what happened to them, is a note waiting
to be written (`items-and-texts.md`).

**Hook:** a wet cloth mask (linen and a stoppered water skin) as a craftable that shortens choking,
and dries out with use.

## 3. The six weathers

The Smothering has six weathers, and everyone in the Blackfen knows them by sight, sound and taste
before they arrive. They follow one another in an order the old say never changes:

> *Fog follows ash, sleet follows fog, frost follows sleet. The red wind breaks the frost, and after
> the wind, the calm. Then ash again.*

That rhyme is the Godot build's cycle (Ashfall, Miasma fog, Sleet, Hoarfrost, Cinder-squall, Dead
Calm). In the world a weather holds for hours or days; the game compresses a spell to 70 to 130
seconds, with an 8 second blend between them, so the rhyme is a rule of thumb, not a calendar.

What each weather does in the Godot build, against Ashfall as the ordinary night:

| Weather | Sight (m) | Hearing | Torch burn | Shelter fire | Footing | Spore clouds last |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ashfall | 24 | x1.0 | x1.0 | x1.0 | 100% | x1.0 |
| Miasma fog | 7 | x0.7 | x1.0 | x0.9 | 100% | x1.3 |
| Sleet | 14 | x0.85 | x1.8 | x0.75 | 85% | x0.6 |
| Cinder-squall | 9 | x0.6 | x1.4 | x1.2 | 95% | x0.4 |
| Hoarfrost | 32 | x1.25 | x1.2 | x1.0 | 80% | x0.9 |
| Dead Calm | 40 | x1.6 | x0.8 | x1.0 | 100% | x1.6 |

Hearing multiplies every sound, the survivor's and every enemy's: what muffles a goblin's footsteps
muffles yours. Sight stacks with the survivor's background (the Fen Healer's night-blindness, the
Gutter-kin's sharp eyes) and with choking. Footing slows walking, sprinting and blocking. A fresh
Pitch Torch holds 240 seconds of fuel at x1.0.

Who comes out and who goes to ground in each weather is in the bestiary's table (`bestiary.md`,
*Weather and the bestiary*); this file does not repeat it.

### Ashfall

**What it is like.** The ordinary night. Grey flakes come down slow and a little sideways, the mist
lies low over the ground, and the moon is a smudge. Sound carries as it always has. You can see a
torch across the yard and a figure at a good stone's throw, about 24 m, before the ash and the dark
take it. The flakes hiss faintly on a torch and settle on your shoulders.

**What it does.** Nothing beyond the ordinary: this is the measure every other weather is held
against.

**What people do.** Everything. Roofs are swept, water skimmed, scavenging done. A night of plain
ashfall is a working night, and the Watchhouse sends people out in it more than in any other.

**Folk names.** *The ordinary.* *Grey weather.* *The sift.* Hedge-priests call it **the Keeper's
blanket**, half in earnest and half not; chapel cleaners who swept it out of naves for thirty years
call it other things.

**Beliefs.** That ash tasting of iron means a squall coming; that ash falling straight down means
the calm is near. Neither is reliable; both are repeated.

**The danger.** That it is ordinary. People stop watching in ashfall. Most of the book's goblin
raids on stores happen in it, and most of its falls from roofs.

**Hook:** before a change of weather, a short sensory cue in the banner area (the taste of iron, the
flakes falling straight) that a careful player learns to read.

### Miasma fog

**What it is like.** It rises off the marsh, not out of the sky: first a green-grey layer to the
knee, then the chest, then everything. It smells of rot and standing water. The few flakes that
still fall clump in it and drop fast. The moon goes out. Sound dies: footsteps, voices, even a
blade on a shield come through it dulled, as if from under a blanket. You cannot see past a few
paces.

**What it does.** Sight closes to 7 m; a night-blind healer sees about four paces. Every sound
carries seven-tenths as far, yours and theirs. The shelter fire burns a little lower. A husk's cloud
hangs longer, x1.3: a death burst that would last eight seconds in ashfall lasts more than ten.

**What people do.** Stay in, or go out roped together. Scavengers walk with a hand on the wall or
on the shoulder in front, and speak in a set of short calls ("here", "still", "back") so nobody is
lost three paces from the door. Doors are marked with a hung pot or a bell with its tongue tied
(rung by hand, once, only to guide someone in). Nobody lights a cooking fire in fog if they can
help it, because the smoke lies on the ground and tells everything nearby where the food is.

**Folk names.** *The green.* *Fen-breath.* *The coughing fog.* Old healers still say **miasma**,
the word from before for bad air that carries sickness, and it stuck.

**Beliefs.** That the fog carries the cough; that it comes up thickest over the common pits; that
the dead walk in it. The first may be partly true (spore clouds do hang in it), the second is true
of every low place, and the third is how fen folk describe the Ashbound wandering off their ground,
which they do in fog (`bestiary.md`).

**The danger.** You hear things late and close. In fog, a husk is upon you before its smell is, and
when it bursts the cloud does not move. Keep the torch lit in the hollow and step back from
whatever you kill.

### Sleet

**What it is like.** Grey rain half frozen, driven slant and fast, rattling on roofs and hissing on
any flame. It beats the ash out of the air and onto the ground, where it turns to a grey slurry
that coats boots and makes every slope a slide. You can see about half as far as in ashfall. The
noise of it on stone and water covers smaller noises.

**What it does.** The torch gutters and burns x1.8: a fresh torch lasts a little over two minutes
instead of four. The shelter fire sinks to three-quarters of its light. The ground is grease: you
move at 85%. Sight is 14 m. Sound carries 0.85 as far. Spore clouds are beaten down fast, x0.6.

**What people do.** Carry the torch under the cloak and light it only at need. Walk short and flat,
feet wide. Keep the fire fed whatever it costs, because a fire that goes out in sleet is hard to
start again with wet kindling. Every roof is swept the moment it stops, because ash plus sleet is
the weight that brings them down (section 1). Sleet is also when stores are checked, because the
ash-rats come indoors.

**Folk names.** *The grease.* *Grey rain.* *The torch-drowner.* Causeway carters say a cart **goes
to grease** when it slides off the bank, and they mean the weather as much as the road.

**Beliefs.** That sleet "washes the spores out", which is true as far as it goes (section 2) and
leads people to think a hollow is safe the morning after, which it is not: the dust is in the mud.

**The danger.** Light and footing at once. A slip at the wrong moment in a fight, and a torch that
dies in the hand half way across a dark yard. Count your fuel before you go out in sleet, and keep a
second light if you have one.

### Cinder-squall

**What it is like.** A hot, gusting, roaring wind that comes down the fen sideways, full of orange
sparks and the smell of burning reed and peat. The flakes are not grey but glowing; they skitter
along the ground and wink out in puddles. The sky to the north or west goes a dull red. Nothing can
be heard over it, and you can see only a few strides through the cinders, about 9 m.

**Why embers in a fen.** The plain cause, which everyone in the Blackfen knows: the fen is burning,
upwind, somewhere. The reedbeds stand dead and dry above the waterline for most of the year, and a
single spark takes a mile of them. Under the reed is peat, and peat that catches does not go out. It
smoulders underground for months, through rain and frost, creeping along the bed and venting smoke
from holes in the moss. Fen folk call these fires **the underburn**. Some were lit in the first
winters, from cottages left burning when their households fled, and are said to be burning still.
When a dry gusting wind comes down off the uplands it fans the underburn up into the reed, the reed
goes up in sheets, and the wind carries burning fluff, reed-ends and crumbs of glowing peat for
miles. That is a cinder-squall. Why the wind comes hot and dry off the uplands at all, nobody knows;
*guesses* run from the high country's air falling into the fen to the north still being warm under
the ash.

**What it does.** Hearing drops to 0.6: every sound carries little more than half as far, the
survivor's and every enemy's. Sight is 9 m. The torch is fanned and burns x1.4 (under three minutes
for a fresh one). The shelter fire roars and throws more light, x1.2, which is welcome and not safe.
Gusts take a little off your footing (95%). And the wind tears spore clouds apart: x0.4, a death
burst gone in about three seconds.

**What people do.** Wet the thatch and put out every open flame in a building with a straw roof;
keep a bucket by the door. Cover the mouth against smoke. Shout and no one hears, so households use
hand signs. Squalls are good weather for moving unheard, for you and for the things that move under
them (`bestiary.md`), and the best weather for walking into the Spore Hollow, if you must.

**Folk names.** *The red wind.* *The fire-wind.* *Underburn weather.* Peat-cutters say the bank is
**breathing** when the underburn vents before a squall.

**Beliefs.** That a red sky to the west means the Causeway reed is burning and the squall will come
down the road. That an ember that lands on you is luck if it goes out and a death if it does not.
The second is about thatch.

**The danger.** Fire itself, in a shelter with a straw roof. In the open, deafness: you cannot hear
what is behind you, and it cannot hear you, and only one of you will think of that first.

### Hoarfrost

**What it is like.** The ash stops. The sky clears to a hard black with a white moon in it, the
brightest light the Blackfen sees, and the cold comes down like a lid. Frost furs every reed and
rope. Breath smokes. The ground freezes to glass. The air rings: every footstep crunches, every
cough carries across the yard, and a dropped pot is heard at the Watchhouse from the Causeway.

**What it does.** Sight opens to 32 m under the moon. Every sound carries x1.25. The ground is ice:
you move at 80%, the slowest of any weather. The torch burns x1.2, a little over three minutes for
a fresh one; the dry cold draws at the flame. Spore clouds hang a little less long than in ashfall
(x0.9).

**What people do.** Wrap boots in sacking for grip and silence. Walk on the grey verges, not the
glazed road. Speak low indoors as well as out. Hoarfrost is the weather for finding things, because
tracks show in the frost, and for being found, for the same reason. Carters move loads on frozen
ground that would bog them in the thaw, and pay for it on the ice.

**Folk names.** *The glass.* *The ringing.* *Moon-frost.* *The bright cold.*

**Beliefs.** That you should not sleep under the hoarfrost moon in the open. It is sound advice for
other reasons.

**The danger.** You can see far, and so can everything else, and your footing is worst exactly when
you most need to run. The book's advice: in hoarfrost, do not run. Choose ground before you need it.

### The Dead Calm

**What it is like.** The wind stops. Nothing falls. The mist sinks flat and thins to nothing, and the
whole yard stands clear and still to the edge of sight, 40 m and more. Smoke rises straight. The
torch burns tall and steady. There is no sound at all, until there is, and then it is very loud.

**What it does.** Everything is heard by everyone: every sound carries x1.6, the survivor's and
every enemy's. A walk that the troll hears at 6 m in ashfall it hears at nearly 10. Sight is 40 m.
The torch burns slow in the still air, x0.8 (five minutes for a fresh one). Spore clouds hang
longest of all, x1.6: a husk's death burst lasts about thirteen seconds where it fell.

**What people do.** Stop. Households sit still and speak in whispers or not at all. Work that makes
noise waits: no chopping, no hammering, no sweeping. Children are hushed with a hand. If you must
move, you move slowly, on soft ground, and you do not carry anything that clinks. Quarry folk at
Rook's Stair walk on their toes and fear the Calm above all weathers (`bestiary.md`).

**Why the orks fear it.** Orks are beasts, and in the Dead Calm they behave like beasts before a
storm: they go to ground and fall silent (`bestiary.md`, *Quiet*). The pack at the choke point
draws in behind its wall of stones, lies flat, and stops its grunting and snuffling. Herders saw
the same in cattle before thunder in the old years: heads down, still, ears working. If something
blunders in among them they come up all at once and fight, hard and quick and without a sound, then
go flat again. What the book knows is only this behaviour, and that the listeners beneath the hills
keep the same silences in the same weather. What people guess, and the book will not choose:

- *The bestiary's guess:* something below hunts by sound, and the orks fled it. An animal that
  fled a hunter that listens goes still when every sound carries.
- *The miners' guess:* below ground, still air is bad air. Citadel miners feared the moment the
  draught stopped in a gallery, because it meant the air had gone foul; the pit ponies knew it first
  and would not move. Orks came up from deeper than any miner went, and perhaps they feel the
  stopping of the air the way the ponies did.
- *The plain guess:* thirty years of being hunted by people have taught them that the Calm is when
  they are easiest to hear, and so easiest to kill. Rats learn as much.

**Folk names.** *The held breath.* *The stopping.* *The long ear.* Quarry folk call it **stone
weather**, because in it you are safest being stone.

**Beliefs.** That something else comes out in the Calm (the bestiary records the rumour and nothing
more). That you should never be the first to speak when the wind stops. That the Calm is the
Smothering listening. None of it has a witness in the book.

**The danger.** You are heard. A swing carries nearly twenty metres, a sprint more than twenty, and
the blind thing in the quarry is at its deadliest. And a spore cloud you burst stays where you
burst it for a long time. *When the wind stops, so do you.*

## 4. Fire and fuel

### The hearth

The Keeping teaches that the world is held apart from the dark by obligations kept, and *hearths
warm* is one of them (`keeping-and-merewick.md`). Thirty winters have made it literal. The hearth is
where a household eats, sleeps, tends its sick and settles its quarrels; it is the only place warm
enough to dry boots and the only place light enough to mend by. Who sits nearest it is decided
every night, and it is the clearest statement a household makes about who it values: the sick, the
small, the one who was out longest, the one who brought the fuel. At the Watchhouse the argument
about whom to admit is, underneath, an argument about how many the fire can warm.

In the Godot build, the shelter fire is where the survivor starts, and the weather breathes on it:
sleet sinks it to three-quarters of its light, fog to nine-tenths, and a cinder-squall fans it up to
x1.2.

### Peat

The Blackfen's fuel was always peat: cut in turves from the banks, stacked to dry through the summer
and burned all winter. The Smothering broke that cycle where it hurts most. There is no summer to
dry it. Peat is now cut in the thaw, when the banks can be worked, and dried indoors through the
whole year, stacked under eaves, in lofts and along the walls of every room, so that every house is
half a peat-stack. Wet peat smokes and smoulders and gives little heat, but its smoke drives off
ash-moths (`bestiary.md`), and so it is burned on purpose at dusk.

Peat-cutters cough worse than anyone except the sick; the Peat-cutter background's flaw (winded) is
the trade's mark. The underburn (section 3) is the cruel joke of it: peat burning underground for
months, and nobody able to use any of it.

### Timber

The woods died standing in the first winters. For a decade the dead birch of the fen was free fuel;
now the near woods are cut back to stumps and good wood is carried in from farther out. Seasoned
timber is a trade good (the Seasoned Timber item: "dry enough to burn or build with"), and the
choice it carries is always the same one: burn it tonight or build with it for the winter after.
Every chapel roof in the Blackfen has been argued over on those terms (Merewick paid off a debt with
one). Selling green or wet timber as seasoned is the oldest cheat on the roads (`bell-road-
locations.md`, the Split Kiln).

### Pitch and oil

**Pitch** is boiled out of pine stumps and resin in covered pits by tar-burners on the upland edge,
and comes down the Bell Road in kegs. A Pitch Torch is a stave with rag wound round the head and
soaked in it: smoky, spitting, bright enough to see a room by and to be seen across a fen.
**Lamp oil** (the Flask of Lamp Oil) is rendered fat, eel oil and, where the Last Gardens grows
flax, a little seed oil. It burns cleaner than pitch and costs more, and a flask also "douses rags
for fire", which is what most of it is used for.

### Slag-iron stoves

Slag-iron holds heat for hours once fired (fact 2). Before the Breaking, the Citadels sold it south
as firebacks, hearth-plates and closed stove-boxes, and a lord's hall with a slag-iron stove was
warm all night on one evening's firing. In the Smothering these are the most valuable objects in the
north after food and salt. A household with a slag-iron plate behind its fire burns perhaps a third
of the fuel of one without, and sleeps warm.

Fen folk call a fireback **a slag-back** and a stove-box **a heat-box**. Most were left in manor
houses when the lords fled; most have since been carried off or broken up for scrap. Orks den
against a warm one if they find it, and a pack that has found a ruin with a slag-back in it is
very hard to move. Stealing a household's slag-back in winter is
spoken of the way stealing its seed is.

**Hook:** a slag-back in the fallen Causeway tower or the ork plunder pile, too heavy to carry far
(a fixture to recover with help, or a reason for a second player), that halves the Watchhouse's
fuel use once set behind the hearth.

### The cost of light

*A light means fuel, not safety* (`bestiary.md`). Light is the most expensive thing a survivor
spends. A torch burns fuel that could have been heat. It draws ash-moths first and something else
after. The ogre smells cooking from very far downwind, and most ogre deaths start with someone
cooking. Light also shows you to everything that sees, while doing nothing about what hears.

In the Godot build the cost is literal: a Pitch Torch's condition is its fuel, 240 seconds when
fresh, and it dims as it runs low and gutters out. The backgrounds start with between 90 seconds
(the Gutter-kin, who never had the means) and 360 (the Hedge-priest, whose torch is tended like a
vow). The weather spends it faster or slower (section 3). And it is the only answer to a hollow
full of husks.

### The last coal

*Preserve the last hearth coal overnight* is one of the Keeping's customs (`keeping-and-merewick.md`),
and it may be the one most widely kept. Starting a fire from nothing in the Smothering, with damp
tinder and no dry kindling, can take an hour and fail. So nobody does, if they can help it.

- **Banking.** Last thing at night, the fire is raked together and its best coal buried in ash. The
  joke every child is told is that ash is the one thing the Blackfen has plenty of, and it is the
  thing that keeps the fire alive.
- **The coal-keeper.** The last person awake banks the fire and answers for it in the morning. It
  is an office, given to someone trusted; to lose the coal is a shame spoken of for years.
- **Lending fire.** A household whose fire has gone out sends someone with a clay pot or a hollowed
  horn (a **fire-horn**) to a neighbour for a coal. The Keeping holds that fire asked for must be
  given; to refuse fire to a neighbour is the gravest breach of hospitality a household can commit,
  worse than refusing food. A coal lent is owed, and a careful household remembers who it owes.
- **The coal for the dead.** Some households carry a coal from the hearth to the grave in a pot and
  leave it burning on the lime. Some say it is for the dead; gravediggers say it is so the bloom
  knows fire is near. *(Proposed local custom; it does nothing anyone can prove.)*
- **The Last Hearth.** The movement of that name (`keeping-and-merewick.md`) takes the custom as
  far as it goes: it shelters everyone, and forbids anyone to leave in winter, because the labour of
  everyone keeps its fire.

**Hook:** "bank the fire" as a shelter action before resting, and a cold-start penalty (time, a
timber) if the coal is lost.

## 5. Food and water under the Smothering

### What grows

Not much, and only fast. The growing season is the thaw and a few grey weeks after it, between the
last frost and the next, and only what can be in and out in that time is worth the seed.

- **Roots and cabbage** are the dependable harvest (the Last Gardens): turnips, kale, cabbages
  that will stand a frost.
- **Onions** under cover, where someone has cover to give.
- **Flax** for linen, oil and trade.
- **Rye** in sheltered strips, in some years, if the thaw comes early. Wheat and barley have failed
  in the open for most of the thirty years.

Every bed is washed of ash by hand, leaf by leaf. Seed is worth more than food, and is kept locked,
counted and argued over. In a hungry winter the seed enclosure is the last thing eaten, and a
household that eats its seed has decided it will not see another spring.

### What can be eaten

- **Eels** from the meres and drains, smoked, are the fen's best food (`bestiary.md`).
- **Peat-boar**, salted, keeps through the winter.
- **Birds**, eggs, frogs and fish from the meres.
- **Reedroot**: the starchy root of the bulrush, dug from the mud in the thaw, dried and pounded
  into a grey flour. **Fen-bread** made of it is heavy, gritty and filling, and is what most of the
  Blackfen eats most days.
- **Ash-rats**, when it comes to that. Nobody says it has come to that.
- Nettles, cress and pond-greens in the thaw, boiled.

**What is not eaten.** *Never eat anything white that grew from the ground.* No mushroom of any
kind is eaten in the Blackfen any more; the risk that a field mushroom is a corpse-cap has made the
whole family forbidden, and the ogre's refusal to touch spore-growth (`bestiary.md`) is taken as
proof that it knows something. Whether boar that ate the dead are safe is the book's open question.
Most households eat them anyway.

### Salt and preserving

Salt is the money (fact 15), and it is money because it is food that keeps. Meat salted in autumn
is meat in the lean weeks; fish packed in salt is fish in the thaw; cabbage in brine is greens in
midwinter. The Salt Ration item ("preserves meat through the Smothering") is a measure of a
household's winter in a pouch. Salt comes up the Salt Trace from the south, and every grain of it
is carried. Orks crave it the way deer crave a salt-lick, and they raid stores for it; a dead ork
often has salt crusted in its hide from licking it.

Smoking is the other way: eels and boar hung in peat smoke at the top of the chimney, where the ash-
moths will not go. A house with a good smoke-hole is a house that eats in winter.

### Water, and the pot

All water is boiled. Marsh water carries ash grit, the flux, and whatever drowned upstream; standing
water carries bloodwicks (`bestiary.md`). The routine is the same everywhere: stand it overnight so
the ash sinks, pour the clear part through cloth, then boil it. Boiling costs fuel, so water costs
fuel, and the Last Gardens sells boiled water by the measure.

For that you need a pot that will stand a hard fire every day. Clay cracks. Copper was carried
south. Most iron pots were taken for scrap or sold in the first years. A good **Cast-Iron Cooking
Pot** ("boil marsh water and simmer stews at the shelter fire") is a household: with it, a shelter
can make water safe and turn bones, roots and a fist of salt into stew for ten. Without it, the
shelter is a ruin with people in it. The Watchhouse's pot is in the ogre's bone heap (an Act I
objective); ogres keep iron things that smelled of food (`bestiary.md`), so it went the way its last
owners did.

**Hook:** the pot carries a maker's mark or a household's scratched initials; returning it to the
Watchhouse could start a conversation with someone who recognises them.

### Hunger

Hunger is the background noise of the Smothering, and **the lean weeks** are its loudest: the last
of the winter, after the salt meat is gone and before the thaw lets anyone dig, fish or plant. In
the lean weeks people boil leather straps and tallow candle-ends, scrape the inner bark of dead
birch, and cut the fen-bread with sawdust. Fights over food are fights over life.

Hunger also makes people look like the Ashbound: a hungry family on the Causeway has been called
Ashbound and turned away before (`bestiary.md`). Feeding a stranger is the oldest test the
Watchhouse has, and it fails it as often as it passes.

## New names

Proposed in this file, none in either build yet:

- **ash-eye**: the red-rimmed, gritty squint of the Blackfen; an eye clouded by ash.
- **ash-tooth**: grazing animals' teeth worn to the gum and joints swollen from eating ash.
- **the ash-post**: the Watchhouse doorpost notched each spring at the deepest drift; the main
  evidence in the thinning argument.
- **the grey mud**: carriers' name for the thaw.
- **the underburn**: peat fires smouldering underground for months or years; the source of the
  cinder-squall's embers. A bank "breathing" is the underburn venting.
- **the red wind, the fire-wind, underburn weather**: folk names for the cinder-squall.
- **the ordinary, grey weather, the sift, the Keeper's blanket**: folk names for ashfall.
- **the green, fen-breath, the coughing fog**: folk names for the miasma fog.
- **the grease, grey rain, the torch-drowner**; "going to grease": folk names for sleet.
- **the glass, the ringing, moon-frost, the bright cold**: folk names for hoarfrost.
- **the held breath, the stopping, the long ear, stone weather**: folk names for the Dead Calm.
- **slag-back** (a slag-iron fireback) and **heat-box** (a slag-iron stove-box).
- **the coal-keeper**: the person who banks the fire at night and answers for it.
- **fire-horn**: a hollowed horn or clay pot for carrying a lent coal.
- **the coal for the dead**: a coal carried to a limed grave (proposed local custom).
- **reedroot** and **fen-bread**: bulrush-root flour and the gritty bread made from it.
- **the lean weeks**: the end of winter, after the salt meat and before the thaw.
