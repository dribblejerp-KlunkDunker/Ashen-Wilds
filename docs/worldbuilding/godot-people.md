# The Godot game's people

**Status: Proposed. Godot addition, 2026-11-05.** Nothing here is canon until the owner says so.
Precedence as in `CANON.md`: the owner's decisions, then what ships in the Godot game, then the
canon sheet, then this file.

**Summary.** The people a survivor meets in the Godot build of Ashen Wilds, and the people a
survivor can be. Edda, the woman left bound in the Watchhouse, in depth: her past, why the band
left her alive, the cudgel, her voice, her secret and how she reacts to each of the eight
backgrounds. The eight backgrounds as lives in the Blackfen, each with a loss, a belief, a line
for the fire and a hook for a personal quest. The band that calls itself Ashbound, with a leader
and four members, where they went and how they come back. Six future companions for Acts I to III,
each with a mechanic hook in the game's own terms (noise, sight, fire, carry weight, bandages) and
a reason to leave. Six named people of the Watchhouse refuge. A list of every new name closes the
file.

## Read this first

- **Only humans are people.** By the owner's decision of 2026-11-05, which overrides the archive
  and the canon sheet wherever they disagree, goblins, orks, ogres, trolls and every other
  creature are animals or monsters. They have no language, names, faith, bargains or society.
  Every companion and every named person in this file is human. Where the archive's goblin
  household (the Gutter Kin of Halwen House) or its ork households appear elsewhere, this file
  does not use them, and the Gutter-kin background below is a human waif with no goblin
  connection. That settles open question Q1 of the canon sheet as option (a) without a goblin
  household; nothing here needs a goblin body.
- **The archive's habit holds.** Each entry keeps *what is known* apart from *what people say*.
  Nobody in this file knows what lies below the Vaults or what the orks fled. Nobody is chosen.
- **In-game lines** are plain ASCII: straight quotes, hyphens, no symbols. They are written to be
  pasted into banners as they stand.
- **Acts II and III** are assumed to climb the Bell Road toward the Citadels, as
  `acts-two-and-three.md` proposes. Where this file places a person in a later act it says so;
  the acts file has the final word on order.
- **Names.** Edda is not the Merewick **Eda** who dried pages. No one here is Mara Venn or Harl,
  in either of their versions.

### Before

The Old Causeway is not all stone. Where the raised road crosses the softest ground it is timber:
oak stakes driven into the fen in rows, hurdles laid between them, gravel and rubble over the
hurdles. Timber rots. For as long as anyone remembers, the families of Sallow End were paid by the
Crown, and after S.3 by the unpaid Causeway Watch, to pull rotten stakes and drive new ones. The
Watch paid in salt, from its own stores, because a road that sinks is a road nobody can hold.

Edda grew up in that work. Her mother made hurdles; her father drove stakes with a beetle, the
two-handed wooden maul of the trade, until a section gave way under him in S.6 and the fen kept
him. By twelve she could drive a stake straight. By twenty she knew every timber section between
the Watchhouse and the choke point by the sound it made under a cart: which held, which only
looked as if it held, and where the old line of the road ran before the stone was laid, drowned
now under the reeds but still walkable at low water if you knew the stakes.

She also kept a passing place. The Causeway has wide bays where carts wait for each other; the
Watch kept fires and rope at some of them and a cache of salt buried at a few, so a patrol caught
out in a squall could eat. Edda kept the fire at the one the Watch called the **Cold Passing**,
two miles short of the tower, for nine winters. She never married. When her sister died of the
grey cough in S.17 she took in her sister's boy, **Col**, then four.

The Watch's salt stopped coming when the tower started losing men, years before it fell. Sallow
End went on mending the road anyway, because the road was the only thing between the hamlet and
the fen, and then, after the tower fell in the autumn of S.29, there was no one left to mend it
for. The hamlet broke up over the winter. Some went to Merewick. Edda set out for the Watchhouse
with Col and three neighbours, because the Watchhouse still kept a fire.

Col did not come. He had gone in the spring with a band in grey rags who had come down the
Causeway handing out food. He was sixteen, and they fed him.

### How the band took her

*What is known.* Early this winter a party of four from Sallow End did not reach the Watchhouse.
Two of them, an old man and his daughter, walked in a week later with nothing: no boots, no salt,
no packs. They said grey rags had come out of the reeds at a passing place, taken everything,
let them go at the reed edge, and kept Edda. Then Edda was found bound in the Watchhouse hall,
with goblins nesting in the rooms beyond her.

*What Edda says,* once she trusts you, and not all at once:

- The band needed someone who knew the drowned line of the old road, because the orks at the
  choke point have made the Causeway impassable and the band wanted to be on the far side of it.
  At night, at low water, she walked them round the choke point on stakes she had driven herself.
- In return she asked for Col. The band's leader, the man they call the Doleman, said Col could
  choose. Col chose to stay with them.
- On the way back they made her show them the Watch's salt at the Cold Passing.
- Then they brought her to the Watchhouse, tied her to the hall post and left her.

### Why they left her alive and bound

*What people guess.* Every one of these is told at the Watchhouse fire, and the book will not
choose.

- **The verdict.** The band calls itself Ashbound because the word is a verdict: what the
  Watchhouse calls someone after it has stopped helping them. The band has its own use of it.
  They do not kill a person who has served them. They tie that person where the Blackfen will
  decide: cold, goblins, or whoever comes by. They call it *leaving them to the ash*. It lets them
  say they are not murderers. Edda believes this one.
- **Col.** The boy would not let them kill her. Tying her where the refuge would find her was the
  Doleman's way of letting Col believe she would live.
- **A message.** The Watchhouse turned the band's people away once, years ago (see section 3).
  A woman left bound in its own hall says: *this is what your door is for.*
- **Bait.** A captive draws the refuge's people into a hall full of goblins. The band waits for
  others to do the dangerous work, then takes what they gathered. If so, the band is coming back.

### The cudgel

The band took every edge from her: knife, billhook, the iron-shod beetle. A cudgel is not worth
taking. Nobody sells a stick and nobody kills you for one. Hers is a length of seasoned oak
hurdle-stake she cut and shaped herself, and when she was tied it was thrown into the corner of
the hall where she could see it. Cut free, she picks it up before she says anything else.

She would not take a blade now if you gave her one. She says a stick does not stick in a rib,
does not need an edge kept, and a stake-hand already knows the swing. The truer reason is in
*What she hides*: she does not want to be able to kill someone by accident. (In game: 14 damage
and 12 poise a blow, slow to start, slow to recover. It is a stake-driver's swing, not a
fighter's.)

### How she speaks

Plain and short. Fen words: *hold* (stand fast), *soft* (ground that will not bear), *the wet*
(the fen), *a passing* (a passing place). She names a debt plainly and pays it. She does not
swear by the Keeper; she touches the lintel when she goes under one, because her mother did. She
never says Col's name to a stranger. She does not say *Ashbound* about anyone she has not seen
up close.

### What she wants

To pay the debt and be done with it. She says *I owe you* and means a measure: your back kept
until you are safe, then she is her own again. Underneath, she wants Col back, and she wants him
to choose to come, which she knows is not the same thing. She wants the Causeway mended. She will
not say this aloud, because nobody is going to mend a road again.

### What she hides

Three things, in the order she would admit them.

1. **The salt.** The Watch's cache at the Cold Passing was the refuge's winter margin. The
   Watchhouse's stores-keeper counts on it (see Joan Tallis, section 5). Edda showed the band where
   it was buried. She has said nothing, and every day she eats at the Watchhouse fire she is eating
   from the hole she made.
2. **Col.** One of the grey rags is her sister's boy. If the band comes back, he comes with it.
3. **Why she will not carry an edge.** On the night walk round the choke point, a man in the
   band slipped off a stake into the soft. She was nearest. She could have held out the cudgel.
   She did not, and he went under, and nobody in the band saw. She does not know whether she
   froze or chose. She will not carry anything that would make the next time easier.

### Across the acts

*Proposed, for whoever builds her story.*

- **Act I: a debt.** She follows because she owes you. She asks nothing and offers no past. Small
  tells only: she knows which Causeway timbers bear, she flinches from the Cold Passing, she will
  not sit with her back to the hall post. If the refuge finds the salt cache gone, she is the
  first to say the Watch must have moved it.
- **Act II: the band again.** On the far side of the choke point the band's trail is her trail.
  The secrets come out, by her own word if you have lifted her twice or more, or by Joan's
  reckoning or Col's face if you have not. The refuge may want her gone. What you do then decides
  whether she stays because she owes you or because she chooses.
- **Act III: her own.** If the debt is called paid and she stays, she stops saying *I owe you*.
  Her last decision is about Col, and it is hers, not yours: bring him home, let him go, or stand
  between him and you.
- **Mechanic growth, kept small.** She never gets a pack or stamina. Two proposals that suit her:
  in HOLD she reads the ground, so a held Edda stands one pace further out without losing her
  leash (she knows where the soft is); and once her secrets are out she will carry one clean rag
  of her own, used on herself when she goes down if you cannot reach her in time. Both are
  levers, not promises.

### Edda and the eight backgrounds

Her first lines are the same for everyone. What follows is how she warms, or does not.

- **Watch Deserter.** The Watch paid Sallow End in salt, then stopped. She knows the tower fell
  and knows someone ran. She is slow to warm and sharp when she does. *"You lot kept the road. My
  father died keeping it for you. Don't tell me about holding."* If you go back to the tower, she
  goes with you, and that is how she forgives.
- **Cinder Factor.** Carriers and stake-hands knew each other at every passing; she trusts you
  at once and talks shop. *"You'll have walked my timbers. Did they hold?"* She is the first to
  tell a Factor which embankments are soft now.
- **Chapel Cleaner.** She is wary of anything that smells of the Keeping's rules, then sees you
  sweep a corner of the hall without thinking, and likes you for it. *"You tidy as if somebody's
  coming back."*
- **Peat-cutter.** Fen folk both; she listens to your breathing on the first long walk and says
  nothing. Later: *"My sister coughed like that for years before it was anything. Some never get
  worse."* She is kinder to you than to anyone, and you do not know why until you learn about her
  sister.
- **Gutter-kin.** She has known waifs; the band took Col by feeding him. She watches your hands
  around the stores and then stops watching. *"You were someone's boy once. Or girl. Somebody
  should have fed you."* She is harder on you than she means to be, because you are what Col
  could become.
- **Hedge-priest.** The least easy pairing. She does not hold with priests, ordained or not, and
  a sworn-hands oath strikes her as a luxury. *"You won't hit hard. Fine. I will. Somebody has to."*
  If you keep the light lit through a sleet night for her, she brings you the last coal herself.
- **Fen Healer.** She owes you before you cut her free; you are the one who looks at her wrists
  first. She lets you near her when she is down and nobody else. *"Your eyes are bad. Mine aren't.
  Walk on my side."* She becomes your eyes at dusk, and says so.
- **Causeway Carter.** You and she worked the same road from opposite ends. She calls your cart a
  load on her timbers and you call her timbers a hazard to your wheels, and both of you enjoy it.
  *"Your good ear's on the wrong side. Stand left of me."*

## 2. The eight backgrounds as people

Each card in the start screen is a walk of life the Blackfen already knows. What follows is one
person in each: what that life was, what this survivor lived through, what the kit, trait and
flaw mean in the world, what they believe, whom they lost, a line for the fire and a hook for a
personal quest. The survivor is never named here. The people they lost are.

### Watch Deserter

*"You held the Causeway tower until you didn't. The sword is the one you were issued."* Drilled:
blocking costs a quarter less stamina; the old coif is in the shelter stash. Old wound: 90 health.
Kit: an arming sword in fair condition, a wooden targe, a pitch torch, two clean rags.

**The walk of life.** The Causeway Watch was the Crown's road garrison, left without pay or orders
when the court sailed in S.3. It kept the Old Causeway for twenty-six years anyway: tolls in kind,
passing-place fires, salt for the stake-hands, a tower at the choke point. Its people were
soldiers' children, debtors who joined for bread, farm sons from the fen margin, and fewer every
winter. Nobody issued them anything new after S.3; a Watch sword was a sword someone before you
had carried.

**This survivor.** Joined at sixteen, in about S.12, under **Serjeant Aud Merrick**, who ran the
tower for the last eleven years of its life. Drilled on the targe because there was never enough
mail. The old wound is a spear-thrust under the left arm from a toll riot in S.18, when a crowd of
starving travellers tried to rush the tower's stores; it closed wrong. In the cold it pulls, and a
hard blow drives the breath out of you faster than it should (that is the 90). Last autumn the
orks came over the tower wall on a night of cinder-squall. On the last night Merrick sent someone
down the culvert toward the Watchhouse for help that did not exist. The survivor says it was
them. Nobody else alive saw.

**The kit.** The sword is the one issued in S.12, a dead man's before that; the survivor has
kept it oiled through everything. The coif in the shelter stash was left there on an earlier
supply run, when the tower still sent people to the Watchhouse. Drilled hands block without
thinking.

**What they believe.** That the Watch kept its oath after the Crown broke its own, and that this
mattered. That they themselves broke it, maybe. That a road kept is the last thing in the north
that is plainly good.

**Whom they lost.** Merrick, fate unknown. **Pim Haddow**, the tower-mate who shared their watch
for six years, last seen on the wall with a torch. The tower.

**At the fire.** *"Twenty-six years without pay. We held it because nobody told us to stop. I'm
still waiting for somebody to tell me."*

**Hook: the last order.** The tower's muster roll was kept in Merrick's chest in the tower
cellar, and the serjeant wrote every order in it. Beyond the broken choke point, the fallen tower
still stands, roofless, with the Watch's dead inside it. Going back means burying them (lime the
dead or meet them) and finding the roll. The last page will say whether the survivor was sent or
ran. Or the last page will be missing, torn out by somebody, and the survivor will have to decide
what they were without it. The band's Wynn Carrow (section 3) served under Merrick too, and may
have the page.

### Cinder Factor

*"A road carrier. You have walked every embankment of the Blackfen with a load on your back."*
Packhorse: carries 26 kg before slowing, and a full pack clatters less. Bad knees: sprint at 85%,
a dodge costs 30 stamina. Kit: a boar spear, a long pitch torch, two clean rags.

**The walk of life.** Before the Breaking, the Cinder Factors were the agents who moved slag-iron
cinder, the dense unworked bloom, down from the Citadels on pack-trains to the river boats. When
the Bell Road became too dangerous for trains and too poor for carts, the Factor houses turned to
carriers on foot: people who could take forty measures of salt or a crate of needles along an
embankment where no cart could go. A carrier walks under a Factor's mark and is paid on delivery;
the Factor's tally says what was carried, by whom, and to whom it is owed. The Salt Trace is
their road above all; they know which crossing takes which load. (*Proposed origin for the name;
the archive says only that Cinder Factors are road carriers.*)

**This survivor.** Carried for **Factor Lisle Gant** of Brackenreach for nineteen years. Learned
to pack so nothing moves: soft goods round hard ones, iron wrapped in rag, nothing hung outside
the frame (that is why a full pack clatters less). The knees went the way all carriers' knees go,
one embankment step at a time. They can walk all day. They cannot run, and a sudden throw to the
side costs them more than it costs anyone younger.

**The kit.** The spear is the carrier's pole: a walking staff on the embankments, a prop for the
pack when you rest standing up, and a point for Greymaw and boar. The torch is long because a
carrier who runs out of light on the Salt Trace drowns. The rags are always clean; a carrier's
pack has a pocket for them on top.

**What they believe.** A load is a promise. You carry it, or you carry the news of why not. A
Factor's mark is worth more than a lord's word because the Factors still pay.

**Whom they lost.** **Ned Coppin**, their carrying partner, who went into a broken crossing on
the Salt Trace in S.27 with a full load of salt. They could not reach him. They delivered his
load as well as their own, and were paid for both, and have never told his widow that the second
payment was his.

**At the fire.** *"Every embankment from here to the salt landings. I know them by the soles of
my feet. Ask me about any of them except the one."*

**Hook: the second payment.** They still carry Gant's tally for that run, with both loads marked
delivered. Ned's widow, **Hanna Teague**, lives at Brackenreach's Upper Row, and Gant is dead or
gone south, nobody is sure. The quest is a carry: forty measures of salt, earned in the Blackfen,
walked to Brackenreach and put in Hanna's hands with the truth. Salt is weight (0.4 kg a ration),
and 26 kg on bad knees through Greymaw country is the whole quest. Hanna may not want the salt,
or the truth, or either.

### Chapel Cleaner

*"You swept a chapel floor for twenty years and know where everything is kept."* Quiet hands:
searches take a third less time, and your steps carry less far. Untrained: attacks cost a third
more stamina and wear a weapon twice as fast. Kit: a wooden targe, the longest of the common
torches, four clean rags. No weapon.

**The walk of life.** Every chapel of the Keeping had someone who swept it, trimmed its lamps,
washed its cloths, kept its keys and knew which board creaked. After the senior clergy sailed,
many chapels kept going on their cleaners alone: the cleaner knew the order of service, the
places of the dead, where the oil and the candles were kept, and who was owed what. Some became
keepers of the register; some were accused of making themselves priests. (Merewick has one such
woman; this survivor is not her.)

**This survivor.** Swept **Saint Ebbe's at Wick Hollow**, a fen-edge chapel two days east of the
Watchhouse, from S.10 to the autumn of S.29, under **Father Anselm Crewe**, who stayed when his
superiors left and then, in S.22, left too, on a night, with the silver lamp. The cleaner kept the
chapel open for seven more years. They know where everything is kept because they kept it: they
open a chest the way they opened the vestry press, in the order things were put in, without a
sound. Twenty years on a chapel floor in soft shoes, past sleeping penitents, is why their steps
carry less far. They never held a weapon. They swing with their whole body, the edge turns, the
blade bites wood and stone, and a sword in their hands blunts in half the time.

**The kit.** The targe hung in the chapel above the door, an offering to the soldier-saints who
held gateways; the cleaner took it down the night they left, because a chapel with no one in it
does not need a shield and a cleaner on the road does. The rags are altar cloth, boiled. The
torch is the chapel's pitch and tallow, dipped three times, the way they dipped lamp-wicks.

**What they believe.** The Keeping without the priests: a roof kept, a hearth kept, the dead named.
Whether the Keeper exists, they do not know, and they have stopped needing to.

**Whom they lost.** Their son **Tobin**, buried in Saint Ebbe's yard in S.19, of the flux. The
congregation, one by one. Father Crewe, who might still be alive somewhere, which is worse.

**At the fire.** *"I know where the oil is, where the rope is, and where the dead are. I always
know where the dead are."*

**Hook: the older sheet.** Saint Ebbe's register was not only Wick Hollow's. The Watch lodged a
copy of its admittance roll there: who the Watchhouse took in, winter by winter. The cleaner knows
something no one at the Watchhouse knows: the roll was rewritten. Households marked *admitted*
and later *departed* were first written on an older sheet as *refused*. (The archive's
"Watchhouse-area artifact" is this sheet.) The older sheet is still in the vestry press at Wick
Hollow, unless someone has taken it. Bringing it back to the Watchhouse means telling Tamsin Reeve
(section 5) that her book is built on a lie, and telling Sefton Rudd who wrote it.

### Peat-cutter

*"Your arms were made by the fen. The axe was for roots; it serves for other things."* Strong
back: carries 23 kg, and heavy strikes deal 15% more. Winded: 80 stamina, and it comes back
slower. Kit: a woodsman's broadaxe, a short torch, one clean rag.

**The walk of life.** Peat is the Blackfen's fuel. Cutting gangs work banks in the high fen by
day, in summer when there was a summer and in the grey thaw now: slice the turves with a slane,
stack them to dry, carry them in. The axe is for the bog-oak, the black roots and drowned trunks
that lie in the peat and stop a slane dead. A cutter's arms and back are the strongest in the
fen. A cutter's chest is the worst: years of peat-dust, wet feet and the smoke of their own fires.

**This survivor.** Cut the **Moss Hythe** banks, north of the Watchhouse, with their sister and a
gang of six, for most of their life. They can lift a stack of wet turves no one else can lift,
and bring the axe down through bog-oak in one heavy blow. They are short of breath after any
long effort and slow to get it back. Fen folk call it *the cutters' chest*. Whether it is the
first stage of the grey cough, nobody at the Watchhouse will say to their face, and nobody can
tell. (Ash-lung's first stage can last years, and many never go further.)

**The kit.** The broadaxe is the gang's root-axe, its haft replaced four times. The torch is
short because cutters work by day and walk home before dark; they never needed more. One rag,
because cutters bind a cut with peat-moss and get on with it.

**What they believe.** In the fen, which gives and takes. They leave a turf at the edge of a cut
for what lives in the water, as their parents did, and do not claim it does anything.

**Whom they lost.** **Amis Fallow**, the gang's eldest, who went through a skin of moss into a
sink in S.24. Three others of the gang to the cough. Their sister, **Brede**, is not lost. She
will not come in.

**At the fire.** *"This fire's peat? No. Wood. Wet wood. Give me a week at Moss Hythe and you'll
never burn wet wood again."*

**Hook: the last stack.** Brede still lives alone in the gang's turf hut at Moss Hythe, beside the
stack they cut in the last thaw: enough dry peat to keep the Watchhouse hearth through the winter.
Fuel nobody has is the Watchhouse's oldest problem. Brede will not give the stack to a refuge
that argues every winter about letting coughing people in, because she coughs too. The quest is
the carry (dry peat is heavy, and the cutter's 23 kg is the reason they can do it at all) and the
argument: bring Brede in, which means getting Sefton Rudd to open the door to a cougher, or leave
her and take the peat, or leave both.

### Gutter-kin

*"You grew up under the Roofed Mile taking what wasn't watched. The sword was someone else's."*
Soft-footed: your steps carry a third less far, dodges cost 16, you see a little further. Frail:
75 health, and a wound bleeds half again as long. Kit: an arming sword in poor condition, a short
torch, two clean rags.

**The walk of life.** *Gutter-kin* is an old Causeway word for road waifs: children with no house,
who sleep under bridges, in culverts and in the gutters of the roads, and live on what travellers
do not watch. They are not anyone's kin. The name means the gutter is. In the first winters of the
Smothering there were many; most died; some grew up. The Roofed Mile, the avalanche gallery on the
Bell Road, sheltered a gang of them for years: a roof against the ash, a dry floor between the
piers, carts passing slowly enough to rob.

**This survivor.** Left on the Bell Road as an infant in about S.4, by whom nobody knows, and taken
in by the gallery waifs. Grew up in the dark between the piers, where you learn to see by the
ash-light from the arches and to walk without a sound on the masonry (that is why they see a
little further and their steps carry so little). They learned to move out of reach before a hand
closes, because being caught meant a beating or worse. They grew up hungry. Childhood without
enough food leaves its mark: thin bones, soft gums, and wounds that close slowly and bleed long.
Fen healers know the sign. That is the frailty.

They left the Mile when the sheep-drags began across the road at night and the oldest of the gang
went up into the roof space to see and did not come back. That was before the board went up at the
Watchhouse: *ROOFED MILE. Occupied.* The survivor believes they know what is up there. They have
never seen it.

**The kit.** The sword came off a dead man on the Bell Road, below the gallery, whose name they
never learned. It was old and nicked when they took it and they have never had the means to keep
it. The short torch is what a waif can steal. Two rags, from a line in somebody's yard.

**What they believe.** That nothing is given. That a thing nobody is watching belongs to whoever
needs it. That you never sleep across a doorway, because whoever is beyond it may need out more
than you need in. (A waif's rule; the Watchhouse repeats it without knowing where it came from.)

**Whom they lost.** **Fesk**, the oldest of the gallery gang, who taught them to walk soft and
went up into the roof. The rest of the gang, scattered down the Bell Road. Nobody before that,
because they had nobody before that.

**At the fire.** *"First fire I ever sat at where nobody asked what I'd taken. Don't start now."*

**Hook: the gallery cache.** The waifs kept a cache in the gallery, behind a loose stone at the
fourth pier: everything they ever took that was worth keeping, and the scratched marks that were
the only record of who had been one of them. Fesk's mark is the last. In Act II, the survivor can
go back to the Roofed Mile, where carriers no longer shelter and something drags carcasses
between the piers, for the cache and to learn what happened to Fesk. Soft feet and sharp eyes
matter more here than anywhere. The archive keeps the gallery's animal unidentified, and so does
this hook: the survivor finds signs, a cache, perhaps a mark, never an answer.

### Hedge-priest

*"No chapel will have you, so the road is your chapel. You keep the light lit."* Steady: 50 poise,
and stamina returns a quarter faster. Sworn hands: you will not strike a heavy blow. Kit: a wooden
targe, the longest-burning torch of any background, three clean rags. No weapon.

**The walk of life.** Hedge-priests are the Keeping's unlicensed: curates never ordained,
penitents who began to preach, priests struck off by superiors who sailed south. They walk the
roads, bury the dead the parishes cannot reach, witness oaths and marriages that no register will
hold, and keep a light at night for whoever is on the road. Some are good. Some take food for
blessings that are worth nothing. The local faithful are glad of them; what remains of the
hierarchy calls them a fraud.

**This survivor.** Was a curate at **Saint Leof's at Crake Ford** in the first winter, S.1, a
young assistant to a priest who sailed. In the worst of the cold the curate opened the chapel to
everyone on the road, as the Keeping said they must. A fever and a grey cough went through the
crowd. Half of those inside died before the thaw. The houses that had kept their doors shut
mostly lived. The survivors who lived barred the curate from Crake Ford, and every chapel that
heard the story did the same. Thirty years on the road since.

The sworn hands are an oath sworn against stone at Crake Ford's ford-stone the morning they left:
never again to add to the dead by their own hand. They will guard, push, turn a blow with the
targe and strike to stop someone. They will not strike to kill, and a heavy blow is a killing
blow. Steadiness is thirty years of standing in the cold through vigils for the dead, and breath
kept slow for prayers.

**The kit.** The torch is the point of them. A hedge-priest keeps the light lit, so they make
their own: pitch, tallow and tow, dipped and dried four times, a torch that burns twice as long as
a carrier's. The targe is a gift, the one weapon a sworn hand will carry. The rags are for the
dying, mostly.

**What they believe.** That the obligations hold whether or not the Keeper does. That they were
right to open the door, and that the dead were real, and that both are true at once.

**Whom they lost.** Crake Ford. Fifty-one names, which they still say, in order, on the longest
night.

**At the fire.** *"I opened a door once and half of them died. I'd open it again. Ask me why and
I'll tell you, but you won't like how long it takes."*

**Hook: the fire-keeper.** Ottor Blewitt, who keeps the Watchhouse hearth (section 5), was a boy
of ten in Saint Leof's in that winter. His mother and two sisters are among the fifty-one. He
knows the hedge-priest by face. The quest is not a journey. It is the Watchhouse door: the winter
argument about admitting coughing travellers comes to a head, the refuge asks the hedge-priest to
speak because they are the only priest it has, and Ottor tells everyone what happened at Crake
Ford the last time this priest opened a door.

### Fen Healer

*"You have closed more wounds than you have made. You see poorly in the dark, and know it."* Deft:
bandaging takes half the time and heals twice as much. Night-blind: you see only 60% as far. Kit:
a boar spear, a pitch torch, five clean rags.

**The walk of life.** Fen healers are not physicians and do not claim to be. They set bones, close
wounds, lance, poultice, deliver children, sit with the dying and tell the living which water to
boil. They have opinions about everything the warning book cannot settle: that ash-moths carry the
spores, that ash-rats spread the flux, that wet cloth masks keep the healthy healthy. Some are
right. They walk between hamlets, wading the meres, and are paid in food, if at all.

**This survivor.** Learned from **Grisel Scarth**, an old healer of the meres north of Merewick,
from about S.8, and walked her round for fifteen years after she died. They have closed hundreds
of wounds with a needle and boiled thread, and can bind a bleeding arm while the patient is still
deciding whether to scream. The night-blindness came on slowly, over the hungry years. They know
exactly what it is: a hunger sign, the same one they have seen in a hundred patients, mended by
liver, eggs and greens that nobody has. A healer who cannot cure themselves of a known thing for
want of an egg is the most Blackfen thing they know.

**The kit.** The spear is a wading pole first: a healer crosses meres and needs to know where the
bottom is. It turns boar. Five rags because there are never enough and a healer always carries
more. The torch is ordinary; it does not help much, and they know that too.

**What they believe.** In boiled water, clean thread and the plain cause. That ash-lung is a
sickness, not a sin and not a verdict. That *Ashbound* is a word people use to stop being
responsible, and that it is sometimes, terribly, accurate.

**Whom they lost.** Grisel. Patients beyond counting; they remember the children. A daughter,
**Mab**, who was born small in S.15 and lived four days.

**At the fire.** *"Give me your arm. No, the other one. You've been bleeding into that sleeve for
an hour and calling it sweat."*

**Hook: the two who came back.** The warning book records two people who say they came back from
silence: the third stage of ash-lung, when speech stops and recognition goes. Grisel believed one
of them, and wrote her name in the healer's own book: **Sib Arden**, of the reed hamlets beyond
Merewick. The quest is to find Sib, and the other one if there is another, and learn what they
can say. The answer must stay uncertain: a misdiagnosis, a starving woman who was never sick, a
true recovery nobody can repeat, or a story told to get back inside a door. The healer has to
decide what to write down.

### Causeway Carter

*"Carts and oxen, and a cart-axe for the things that come out of the reeds. One ear went to a
squall."* Hardy: 120 health, a worn gambeson, and wounds bleed a quarter less long. One good ear:
you hear only 70% as far. Kit: a woodsman's broadaxe, a wooden targe, the gambeson (worn), a pitch
torch, two clean rags.

**The walk of life.** Carters own carts and the beasts that pull them, which makes them rich by
Blackfen measures and targets on every road. The Causeway carters hauled what the Cinder Factors
could not carry: timber, stone, grain, lime, whole households. Few draught beasts are left after
thirty years, and most carters now pull their own carts or have stopped. The ones who still have
oxen are known by name along the road, and so are the oxen.

**This survivor.** Hauled the Old Causeway for twenty years with a two-ox cart, from the Watchhouse
to the toll house and the choke point and back, through everything. Weather and work made them
hard to kill: they have had their ribs stove by a wheel, been bitten by Greymaw and come home both
times. The ear went in a cinder-squall on the open Causeway in S.26: the cart turned side-on to
the wind to shelter the oxen, and an ember the size of a thumbnail went into the left ear. The ear
healed shut and never heard again. They have learned to turn their head toward a sound, which is
too slow when the sound is behind them.

**The kit.** The cart-axe is for cutting a beast free of a fouled harness and for what comes out
of the reeds at a cart stopped at a passing place. The targe hung on the cart's tail. The
gambeson is a carter's coat: thirty layers of linen against a Greymaw's teeth and a raider's
knife, worn shiny at the shoulders where the harness rests when they pull the cart themselves.

**What they believe.** That the road belongs to those who use it, and that the Watch was a toll
with a sword. That a beast that works for you is owed fodder and rest before you are.

**Whom they lost.** Their wife, **Maud Kersey**, of the grey cough in S.21. And the oxen,
**Patience** and **Plod**, left yoked at a passing place beyond the choke point on the night the
orks took the tower, when the carter had to run.

**At the fire.** *"Say it again, on this side. No. Never mind. I'll watch your mouth. I'm good at
mouths."*

**Hook: the cart at the passing.** The cart is still out there, beyond the choke point, at the
Cold Passing (Edda's passing). Oxen do not last a winter yoked in the open; the carter knows that
and goes anyway. What they find is decided by what the orks and the Greymaw left. The cart itself
can be saved: it needs a wheel (timber, iron scrap, a day's work) and something to pull it. With a
cart the refuge can haul the heavy things of Act II: Coldbarrow's chain, the Split Kiln's lime,
Brede's peat. The carter can pull it themselves, slowly, at a carry weight no pack allows and a
noise that carries twice as far.

## 3. The band that calls itself Ashbound

### What they are

The Watchhouse word *Ashbound* is a verdict: someone who has stopped answering. Most Ashbound are
the far-gone sick. A few are bands who **take the name**: deserters and raiders in grey rags, who
use the word to frighten and to explain themselves. This is one of them. It is about a dozen
strong when it is together, which is rarely. It works in fives and sixes, carries rope, moves at
night and loots. Its people are healthy, mostly, and well fed by Blackfen measures, because they
eat what others gathered.

The grey rags are a uniform and a lie. Anyone in grey rags with their face wrapped looks like the
sick, and nobody goes near the sick. Travellers drop their packs and run. The band picks the packs
up.

They have a story about themselves, and it is partly true. In the winter of S.19 a party of
deserters, debtors and their families came to the Watchhouse door in a sleet and was turned away:
too many, two of them coughing. Four of them died on the Causeway that night. The survivors took
the name the Watchhouse had used for them as they shut the door. *"Ashbound is what you call
someone after you've stopped helping them,"* the Doleman says. *"We just stopped arguing about it."*
The Watchhouse's own book records a refusal that winter. It does not record numbers.

### Their custom

- **The dole.** Everything taken goes to the leader and is shared out by tally, so much a head,
  the old Watch way. Nobody keeps what they took. This is why the band holds together and why
  they hate the Watch they copy.
- **Leaving them to the ash.** The band does not kill its own, or anyone who has served it. It
  ties them in a place where the Blackfen will decide. Edda is the latest. The Watchhouse has
  found others over the years and not known what it was finding.
- **The knot.** Every one of those left was tied with the same knot, a boatman's hitch from Low
  Harrow that tightens as you pull. A careful survivor who has seen it once will know it again.

### The Doleman

**Corwen Dole**, about fifty. Was the quartermaster of the Causeway tower: the man who kept the
stores and handed out the dole of salt and bread, and wrote it in the tally. Deserted in S.18,
after the toll riot, with three seasons of the tower's salt. He was in the party turned away from
the Watchhouse in S.19 and buried his wife beside the Causeway the next morning.

He is not a brute. He is exact. He keeps the tally in a Watch book with the tower's mark on the
cover. He believes he runs the last honest distribution in the Blackfen: everyone fed, everyone
equal, no one turned away who will work. Everyone else is the cost. He speaks softly and calls
everyone by their full name. He let Col choose, and he meant it, and he knew which way the boy
would choose.

*"I fed him. You'd have turned him away at a door. Tell me which of us is Ashbound."*

### Hild Mossop, the binder

About forty, from Low Harrow, where she kept a boat and ferried people across the drowned town
until the water won. Ties every knot the band ties. She tied Edda to the hall post and tied the
knot so that it would hold a day and then begin to give, if Edda worked at it, which Edda did not
know. Hild has never told the Doleman. She thinks of it as a different verdict.

She is the band member most likely to let someone go, and the most dangerous in a fight, because
she brings rope to it. *"Hold still. It's worse if you pull."*

### Wynn Carrow, the eyes

Thirties. A Causeway Watch archer who deserted the tower in S.25, four years before it fell,
because Merrick was keeping the Watch on the wall when there was nothing behind it to protect.
Wynn knows the Watch Deserter, or knew them on the wall. Watches for the band from high ground
with a bow nobody else in it can draw. Took the last page of Merrick's roll, or says so (see the
Watch Deserter's hook): Wynn went back to the fallen tower in the winter for the Watch's salt and
came out with the serjeant's book under one arm. Wynn has not said what the last page says.

*"You lasted four years longer than me. Did it help anyone?"*

### Tullock, the back

Sixty or so, enormous, slow. A slag-miner from Dunmarrow who came down the Bell Road in S.2 with
the first who fled the Citadels, and has the grey cough. The band keeps him, which is the one
thing everyone who meets them remembers in their favour: a cougher, fed equally by tally, walking
in the middle of the line. He carries the heavy loads, and he will not talk about the mines. He
talks to Col more than anyone. *"Eat. The ash doesn't care if you're hungry."*

### Col Wray, the youngest

Seventeen. Edda's sister's boy. Went with the band in the spring of S.30 because it fed him and
Sallow End was starving. Told the Doleman that his aunt knew the drowned line of the old Causeway.
Argued for her life afterwards. Chose to stay. He is not a monster and not a victim. He is a boy
who has been fed for the first time in his life by people who want something for it, and has
decided he does not mind. He has started wrapping his face like the others.

*"She'd have kept me mending a road for nobody. They gave me a share."*

### Where they are now

Beyond the choke point. Edda walked them round it on the drowned line of the old road, and they
are wintering at **the Drowned Lantern**, a former Causeway inn on the far reach, roofless above
and dry below, with a cellar the water has not reached. The Watch's salt from the Cold Passing is
there, and what the band took from Sallow End's walkers, and what they took from the fallen tower.
They raid the Causeway's far reach and the Bell Road's southern junction, and they are hungrier
than they look, because a dozen people eat a cache fast.

### How they come back

*Proposed; any of these, in any order.*

- **The raid.** They wait for others to do the dangerous work. Once the Watchhouse is secured and
  the stores fill, a night comes when the band crosses back by the drowned line and comes for the
  stores. In game they would be the bestiary's long-promised *looting enemy*: people in grey rags
  who work together, open containers, strip corpses and take items from the shelter stash, and
  run with the loot rather than fight to the death.
- **Rope.** Near the band, a downed companion is not left to bleed: Hild's rope drags them off.
  Edda downed beside the band is not dead and not lifted; she is gone, and the next quest is
  getting her back. (A lever for the companion's DOWNED state, not a promise.)
- **The road.** In Act II, the band's raids on the Bell Road's lower stops are the reason carts
  stop coming to the Last Gardens and Darnel Cross. Their tally book, found, is a list of
  everyone they robbed.
- **The bargain.** The Doleman sends Col to the Watchhouse door with an offer: the band will leave
  the refuge alone for a measure of its stores every month, a dole the other way. Sefton Rudd
  wants to fight. Joan Tallis wants to count. Edda wants to see Col.
- **Col alone.** Late in the winter Col comes back by himself, coughing, the way Tullock coughs.

## 4. Future companions

Six people a survivor could meet in Acts I to III, each human, each with a reason to come, a
system the game already has to hang them on, and a reason to go. The companion code today is
Edda's: follow, hold, fight within a leash, downed and lifted. Each of these adds one lever.

### Dace Ormer, the eel-boy (Act I)

**Who.** Fifteen. An eel-trapper's son from the fen edge whose father did not come back from the
traps in the autumn. Small, quick, watchful, and starving.

**Where met.** Hiding on a ledge in the Old Quarry, where he has been living on rainwater and
whatever the troll leaves. He has learned to keep absolutely still when the troll comes, and he has
been watching the survivor do the same, or fail to.

**How recruited.** Come into the quarry and leave without being pounded. He follows you out at a
distance and comes to the shelter fire on the second night if there is food at the edge of the
light. He has to be fed before he will speak.

**Mechanic hook: ears and eyes.** Dace hears further than you and sees further in the dark. While
he is near, unseen noises raise their ripple from further out (his hearing stacked on yours), and
he calls a source out by direction in a banner (*"Behind you, left."*). He weighs nothing and
fights nothing: in a fight he stays out of reach, throws stones (a light blow that interrupts a
windup), and runs when hurt, coming back when it is quiet. He is the only companion who can go into
the quarry ring without drawing the troll, because he knows how to be still.

**What makes him leave.** Being left. If Edda or anyone else goes down beside you and you do not
lift them, Dace is gone by morning. His father was left at the traps by the men he went out with.

### Agnes Cope, the stone-porter (Act II)

**Who.** Forty-five. A porter of Rook's Stair, the terraced quarry settlement, who has carried
stone and lime up and down its stairs for thirty years. Broad, silent, and very tired.

**Where met.** At the foot of Rook's Stair, where the road is blocked and the settlement is
starving because the stone it trades cannot reach the Bell Road.

**How recruited.** Help clear the blocked road (timber, and a day's work), and she comes with you
as far as you are going, on Rook's Stair's account: the settlement sends her to carry its first
load of stone down to whoever will pay salt for it.

**Mechanic hook: carry weight.** Agnes carries for you. While she follows, part of your pack goes
on her back (proposed: up to 15 kg, chosen in the pack window), so you stay under your own limit,
quieter and faster. What she carries she carries slowly: in FOLLOW she is the slowest companion in
the game, and if you outrun her she drops back and rejoins rather than run. She eats: each rest at a
fire costs a salt ration or she carries less the next day.

**What makes her leave.** Going underground. Her father died in a collapse in the Rook's Stair cuts
when she was fifteen, and she will not go below ground, into a mine road, a culvert or a cellar.
In Act III, at the Citadels' mine roads, she sets down your load at the mouth, square and tidy, and
walks home. She will tell you she will before she does.

### Ivo Pargeter, the lime-man (Act II)

**Who.** Fifty. A lime-burner of the Split Kiln, from one of its three intermarried households.
His brother was the worker killed by an inexperienced correction at the kiln, whose tools nobody
has touched since.

**Where met.** At the Split Kiln, where the working kiln cannot fire because the fuel carriers
stopped coming when the Roofed Mile became a detour.

**How recruited.** Bring fuel to the Kiln (dry wood or peat; Brede's stack if the peat-cutter's
hook went well) so it fires once more. Ivo comes away with a sack of new lime to take to the
Watchhouse for its dead, and stays because there are more dead on the road than he can lime
from the kiln.

**Mechanic hook: lime.** Ivo carries lime and uses it. A spore cloud he walks into he whitens and
kills, like the torch but slower and without the fire; corpse-caps he limes on sight; and the
Roofed Mile animal, the archive says, coughs and draws back from lime in the air, so Ivo is the
companion who makes the gallery passable for a moment, once. The cost is time: he stops to lime
every body you leave, a channel of several seconds that will not hurry, while whatever killed it
may still be near.

**What makes him leave.** Leaving the dead unlimed. If you walk on three times while he is liming,
he lets you go on alone. *"Lime the dead or meet them. I've met them."*

### Wenna Stoke, the penitent (Act II)

**Who.** Thirty. A penitent of the Corpseway: one of those who believe survivors were left behind
to repay a debt, and who retrieve bodies, keep the warnings cut on the stone benches and carry the
sick to the hospice at Saint Orra's Steps. Gentle, tireless and a little frightening.

**Where met.** On the Corpseway, on a resting bench, beside a stretcher whose other bearer has
gone ahead and not come back.

**How recruited.** Carry the other end of the stretcher to the Steps. When it is done she says
the debt is now yours, and comes with you to see it paid.

**Mechanic hook: bandages.** Wenna binds. She uses your clean rags on others without being told:
she lifts a downed companion faster than you can (half the revive time) and heals them more, and
if you stand still near her when you are hurt she binds you too, from your pack. She will also
bind the wounded who are not yours. Near a fight she stops to bind anyone down, including
strangers, and spends your rags doing it.

**What makes her leave.** The door. If you turn anyone away who asks for help (a coughing
traveller at the Watchhouse, a captive you do not cut free, a wounded stranger you walk past), she
says nothing and is gone the next day, back to the Corpseway, to carry them herself. She is also
the companion who will not let anyone else leave once she has saved them, which the refuge will
discover.

### Aldo Flitch and Smut (Act II)

**Who.** Sixty. A kennel-man who kept a fled lord's hounds and let them go wild when the lord left;
the Greymaw on the Blackfen's roads are, some of them, his dogs' children. He kept one bitch, Smut,
who is old now and nearly blind and still the best nose in the fen. Smut is a dog, not a person:
she has no name she knows except the whistle.

**Where met.** On the farmland below the Last Gardens, where Greymaw follow carts. Aldo is walking
behind them.

**How recruited.** Kill no Greymaw while he can see you, or kill them and then lift him when he
goes for you and you put him down. Either way he comes to the fire because Smut will not leave it.

**Mechanic hook: the nose.** Smut smells what no one sees. While she follows, hidden containers
and the stores under rubble show themselves, and an Ashbound lying in reeds or a band in grey rags
waiting on a verge is marked before it rises. The cost is noise: Smut barks. She barks at what
she smells, which tells it where you are, and at night she barks at nothing. Her bark has a reach
in metres like your footsteps and the troll hears it.

**What makes him leave.** Smut's death, after which he goes back to walking behind the Greymaw.
Or a question: who whistles to the Greymaw? People will tell you it is Aldo. Aldo says it is not.
The book does not settle it, and if you accuse him, he leaves.

### Gise Marrable, the lamp-woman (Act III)

**Who.** Sixty-four. A lamp-woman of Greystair, one of the Highland Citadels: she trimmed and
carried the miners' lamps in the deep workings for twenty years before the Breaking, and her
husband **Oswy** was a hewer on the Black Seam, down in the Slagcrown's workings when the Vaults
were opened. She came down the Bell Road in S.1. She has gone back up it now, alone, to see.

**Where met.** At Coldbarrow, below the gate that says *TAKE NOTHING YOU CANNOT CARRY WHEN THE
CART BREAKS*, with a pack she cannot carry any further.

**How recruited.** Carry her pack through the gate (the weight is the price), or leave her to
carry what she can. She comes either way, because you are going where she is going.

**Mechanic hook: heat.** Gise carries a brick of slag-iron in a felt bag. Heated in a fire, slag-iron
holds its heat for hours, and she uses it the Citadel way: she can relight your torch from the brick
without a fire (once per heating), and while she is near, cold weathers bite less (proposed:
Hoarfrost and Sleet slow you less, and the torch burns at its ordinary rate in Sleet). She knows the
mine roads by memory. She is old and the cold slows her badly once the brick is cold.

**What makes her leave.** Going down. Gise wants to go below the Slagcrown's workings to where Oswy
was, and does not believe she will come up. At the mouth of the deepest road she gives you the
brick and goes on alone, unless you stop her, and stopping her is a choice the game should make
hard. Agnes Cope, if she is still with you, sets her load down at the same mouth for the opposite
reason. Neither of them knows what is below. Nobody in this file does.

## 5. The Watchhouse's people

The refuge's people, as they could stand at the shelter fire while the hall is still full of
goblins, and in the hall once it is secured (`act-one-the-outskirts.md` has the order). About a
score in all; these are the ones with a part to play. All of them have an opinion about the door.

### Tamsin Reeve, keeper of the warning book

Sixty. Was a clerk at the Causeway toll house, writing passes, before the toll house became a ruin
everybody wants a roof in. Keeps the warning book (`bestiary.md`) in a dry box under the hall's
one good window, and enforces its habit on everyone: *say what happened, say what you don't know,
write down the dead.* She writes down whatever a survivor tells her, and who told her, and what
they were guessing. She does not believe the Watchhouse's admittance roll is honest (she has never
seen the older sheet; see the Chapel Cleaner), and she is right, and she will need to be shown.

*"Did you see it, or did somebody tell you? Both are worth writing. They're not the same."*

### Ottor Blewitt, the fire-keeper

Forty. Keeps the hearth: banks it at night, keeps the last coal, rations the fuel. Lost his mother
and two sisters at Saint Leof's at Crake Ford in S.1, when a young curate opened the chapel to the
road (see the Hedge-priest). He believes in fire the way other people believe in the Keeper, and
he is the reason the Watchhouse fire has not gone out in nine years. He is kind to everyone except
the one person he will not forgive, and quietest in Sleet, when the fire dims.

*"Fire's not safety. Fire's fuel. Bring me fuel and I'll keep you safe."*

### Sefton Rudd, the door-warden

Sixty-two. A Causeway Watchman of the old tower who lost a leg below the knee in S.14 and was
sent to the Watchhouse to keep its door, which he has done for sixteen years. He decides, or acts
as if he does, who comes in. He was on the door in S.19 when the deserters' party was turned away
in the sleet, and he is the one who said the word. He calls the Watch Deserter a deserter to their
face. He wrote the admittance roll the way it reads now.

*"Thirty beds, forty people, one door. You decide, then. Go on."*

### Joan Tallis, keeper of the stores

Fifty. Counts the salt, the timber, the oil and the rags, and writes every measure in, and every
measure out. Her winter reckoning includes the Watch's salt at the Cold Passing, which she has
never seen but has on the Watch's word, and which Edda gave away. She will find out. She is not
cruel, only exact, and in a refuge that runs out of salt in deep winter exactness is how people
die fairly. She and the Doleman would understand each other very well.

*"Every measure's somebody. Tell me who you want to go without."*

### Cress, the rag-girl

Ten. Washes and boils the refuge's rags in the copper behind the hall, and every clean rag a
survivor carries passed through her hands. An orphan off the Causeway; her mother set out for the
Watchhouse a day ahead of her last winter and never arrived. Cress asks every newcomer whether they
saw a woman with red thread at her cuff on the road. (Merewick's register has an unnamed woman with
red thread at her cuff, buried beside a sluice with a smaller child. The file does not say they are
the same woman. Cress does not know about Merewick.)

*"That rag's mine. I boiled it. Bring it back when you're done bleeding on it."*

### Lew Ashby, the cougher at the door

Thirty-five. A thatcher with the grey cough, first stage, who walked in at the start of the winter.
The refuge would not let him sleep inside and would not send him away, so he sleeps in the lean-to
against the outer wall and chops wood for Ottor. He is the winter's argument with a face. He does
not ask to come in; he has stopped asking. If he is still in the lean-to when the deep cold comes,
he will not be alive in the spring.

*"I don't need in. I need the axe back by morning, that's all."*
