# The Kingdom and the Breaking

**Status: Proposed** (Godot addition, 2026-11-05). Nothing here is canon until the owner says so.
It follows `CANON.md` on every hard fact, and the **owner's rule of 2026-11-05: only humans are
people.** Goblins, orks, ogres, trolls and every other creature are animals or monsters (some of
them cunning) with no language, names, culture, faith, bargains or society. Where the archive or
the canon sheet says otherwise (the Gutter Kin as a goblin household, the Saint Vey's Fold ork
families, trading stones), this file does not use it. "War-clan" survives only as what frightened
people called the ork packs in the first years.

**Summary.** Orlen was a cold kingdom made rich by an ore that keeps heat. The Crown at Wexhaven
held the roads, the tolls and the lords; five hereditary mining houses, the Highland Citadels,
held everything under the highland turf by a two-hundred-year-old charter; the Keeping blessed
both. In S.0 Cerrin Vane, Slag-Lord of the Slagcrown, in debt, pressed by the Crown and unable to
be the Vane who failed, ordered his miners through old worked stone at the end of the Black Seam.
Within the year the north vented ash and summer did not come back. Goblin swarms came out of the
lower workings, then ork packs fleeing something deeper, then the large things. Four Citadels
fell or were abandoned in two years; the fifth, Kell Tor, mortared its own gates and has not been
heard from since. In S.3 King Aldric IV, the court, the treasuries and the Keeping's senior clergy
sailed south, promising relief, and the harbour burned behind them. Then came the Long Dark, the
Holding Years, and the Causeway Watch keeping a road unpaid for twenty-six years, until an ork
pack took the Causeway tower in the autumn of S.29. This file sets out what is known, what is
guessed and who says which. It does not say who built the Vaults or what the orks fled.

## 1. The Kingdom of Orlen before

### The shape of the realm

Orlen runs north from the sea. The **south coast** was the only part rich in the ordinary way:
harbours, salt-pans, walled orchards, and the royal city of **Wexhaven** on its deep-water inlet.
North of it lie the downs and grain country, then the **Blackfen**, then the uplands and the
**highlands** where the Citadels stand. The archive's four roads tie it together: the **Salt
Trace** south to the coast, the **Old Causeway** across the wet, the **Bell Road** north into the
uplands, the **Corpseway** out to Saint Orra's Steps. The north was hard country before the
Smothering. What made it matter to the Crown came down the Bell Road in ore carts.

### How the Crown ruled the north

Through other people's obligations.

- **The lords.** Land was held from the Crown by lords great and small, from the march-lords of the
  downs to estate families like the **Halwens**. A lord owed the Crown **levies** (men for war,
  carts for the roads, grain in a hard year) and owed his tenants shelter, protection and justice
  at his gate. The Keeping called both debts sacred. The Crown collected the first; the lords'
  chaplains enforced the tenants' side of the second.
- **The roads and the Watch.** The Crown kept the roads. The **Causeway Watch**, its road garrison
  (a few hundred at its height), was posted in waystations like the **Wayfarer's Watchhouse**, in
  the **Causeway tower** at the fen end, and at the toll house half way. It kept the road open,
  hanged robbers, pulled carts out of the wet, rang the fog-bells and took the tolls. It was paid
  quarterly in coin from a **pay chest** sent up the Salt Trace.
- **Toll rights.** Every cart on the Causeway paid the Crown, except the Citadels' ore carts, which
  the charter let through free. A peat-cutter's cart paid; a Slag-Lord's twenty carts of iron did
  not, and broke the road the peat-cutters' tolls repaired. It was the fen's oldest grievance.
- **Record.** Royal assize came north twice a year. Between circuits the Keeping's parish registers
  were the only record most people had of their births, debts, rights and dead.

### The Keeping's place

The southern hierarchy at Wexhaven crowned kings and witnessed the charter. Each Citadel kept a
**house of the Keeping** inside its walls, whose abbot blessed each new level before the hewers
entered it. The Keeping held land, lent money, and **held Citadel debt**. Its senior priests called
the slag-iron expeditions *stewardship*: the Keeper had put warmth in the stone so a cold people
could live. Local objections to digging deeper were filed under superstition.

### The five Highland Citadels

Walled mining towns over the pitheads, with smelting halls, granaries, chapels and barracks, and
the shafts going down under all of it. Each was held by a hereditary **Slag-Lord**, who answered to
the Crown for the iron and to no one for what happened underground.

| Citadel | House | Slag-Lord at S.0 | Specialty | Character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **The Slagcrown** | Vane | **Cerrin Vane** | Deep ore, the great smelting halls, the Crown's iron | The greatest and deepest; its spoil heaps ringed the pitheads like a crown. Proud, crowded, in debt. |
| **Greystair** | Tarrant | **Rohese Tarrant** | Stoves: the cast stove plates every northern house wanted | Built down a hillside in terraces; the most like a town; the one that took in refugees and died of it. |
| **Dunmarrow** | Corbet | **Bertram Corbet** | Smithing: blades, the Watch's swords and helmets | A soldier's Citadel, with the largest garrison and the best forges. |
| **Hob's Height** | Haddon | **Adam Haddon** | Bells and charcoal: the foundry that cast the Bell Road's bells | The smallest and highest, named for a miner of legend. Poor, stubborn, devout. |
| **Kell Tor** | Marne | **Ysmay Marne** | Deep pumps and the assay house that stamped iron's grade | The oldest and furthest, on a crag above a black tarn. Exact, private, respected more than liked. Silent since S.1. |

The **Slag-Lords** sat below the march-lords at court and above them in wealth. They intermarried,
quarrelled over seams that ran under two holdings, and closed ranks against the Crown's assessors.
Each Citadel had a **Hewers' Lodge**, the miners' sworn fellowship, whose spokesmen held the old
right to refuse ground they judged unsafe. The charter recognised it; the lords resented it.

### Slag-iron

A dark, heavy ore, almost black when broken and greasy to the touch, that **holds heat for hours
once fired**. Cast or worked, it keeps the property.

- **Stoves.** A slag-iron stove plate fired once in the evening keeps a room warm till morning. In a
  country always short of fuel that was worth more than silver. Greystair plates stood in every
  chapel, inn and hall in the north. Many survivors still own one; some died carrying one.
- **Smithing.** Worked slag-iron is tough and holds a forge's heat so long that a smith can draw a
  blade from one heat. Quenching it was a craft Dunmarrow guarded. Most scrap in the fen was once
  slag-iron goods, which is why it is worth picking up.
- **Warships.** Osbert III refitted the royal fleet with slag-iron ballast, anchors and **galley
  stoves** that let crews winter at sea and fight when southern fleets lay in harbour.
- **Trade.** Plates and bars went south down the Salt Trace and out of Wexhaven for silver, salt,
  oil, wine and cloth. The **Cinder Factors** began as agents carrying slag-iron goods ("cinders")
  to the coast for a share of the price; by S.0 the name meant any licensed road carrier.

Slag-iron made Orlen rich because it was **portable warmth** in a cold world and only Orlen had
it. It also made Orlen depend on the Citadels, the ore carts, and seams that did not last.

### The Deep Charter

In **S.-196** King **Godric II**, needing iron he could not mine himself, granted the **Deep
Charter** to the five highland mining families. As the copies quoted in the fen remember it:

- The Citadels hold **everything below the turf** within their bounds, "the deep and all within it",
  by inheritance.
- The Crown holds the surface, the roads, the tolls and the lords.
- The Citadels pay **the Crown's Fifth**, a fifth of all smelted iron, delivered to Wexhaven, and
  keep the Bell Road; their ore carts pass the Crown's roads **toll-free**.
- The Hewers' Lodges keep **the right of refusal**. A Slag-Lord may set it aside only by taking the
  ground "on his own hazard", paying the families of any dead from his own purse.
- The Keeping witnesses, and blesses each new level.

It said nothing about the deep holding anything but ore.

### Ordinary life

Most people in Orlen never saw a Citadel. **In the fen** they lived on reclaimed land behind dykes
and sluices: rye and barley on the drained fields, peat cut and stacked for winter, reed for
thatch, eels in the sluices, geese and sheep on the higher grazing. Towns like Brackenreach and mill
villages like Merewick sat where a road met water. People paid tithe to the Keeping, rent to a
lord and toll to the Crown, and spent the rest on salt, nails and a better plate. **In the
uplands**, life was sheep, quarry and charcoal: Rook's Stair cut stone, the Split Kiln burned lime,
and the Bell Road's chapels rang in turn, so a message could run from Hob's Height to the fen edge
in an afternoon. Upland families sent their young to the Citadels as hewers and carters, and the
Lodges sent back the dead with their wages and a covenant promise to keep their dependents.

### Remembered kings

- **Godric II** (died S.-181), *the Chartering King.* Granted the Deep Charter. Remembered fondly in
  the Citadels and not at all in the fen.
- **Wynfrith** (S.-151 to S.-119), *the Toll-Queen.* Founded the Causeway Watch (S.-148), raised the
  toll house, built the waystations. The fen cursed her tolls and blessed her road.
- **Osbert III** (S.-82 to S.-44), *Iron Osbert.* Built the slag-iron fleet, opened the southern
  trade, and spent the Fifth faster than the Citadels could deliver it.
- **Aldric III** (S.-44 to S.-15). Paid Osbert's debts by selling offices and raising tolls. In his
  reign the Slagcrown's assayers first wrote that the upper seams were thinning (S.-31).
- **Aldric IV** (crowned S.-15, sailed S.3). Crowned young; lost twelve ships in the great storm of
  S.-7 and spent six years rebuilding the fleet. Called *the Gentle King* before S.0 and other
  things after. Nobody knows where he is.

## 2. The Black Seam and the Breaking

### The debt behind the door

Nothing at the Slagcrown needed a villain. It needed a ledger.

When **Cerrin Vane** inherited in **S.-11**, the upper levels were worked out and the middle levels
thin. The house still owed the Fifth on the old tonnages and still fed four thousand people. His
father had borrowed to cover the gap, from the Crown against future iron and from the **Keeping's
treasury at Wexhaven**, which held twenty years of the Slagcrown's iron as security.

Then the fleet sank. After the storm of S.-7 the Crown needed iron for ships, and in **S.-6** Lord
Treasurer **Aymer Blount** demanded the Slagcrown's **arrears** of the Fifth on a schedule. One line
of his letters is quoted everywhere in the fen: *His Majesty does not doubt the Slagcrown's
ability, only its industry.* The talk at court was of putting the Slagcrown **under Crown
stewardship**: the end of the Vane house in all but name. Vane was thirty-four, head of the oldest
of the five houses, and would have been the first Slag-Lord in two centuries to tell the Crown he
could not pay. Those who write about him agree: he was not stupid, not cruel by the standards of
his kind, and could not bear to be the one who lost it.

### The finding and the wall

In **S.-2** a deep crew under the forewoman **Ebba Cray**, in the lowest level, the **Eleventh
Gallery**, broke into ore so dark and heavy the first lumps were taken for a joke. It assayed
richer than anything in the records and widened as it ran down and east: **the Black Seam**. For a
year it paid. The arrears shrank; the chapel rang for it at midsummer S.-1, and the Abbot of the
Slagcrown's house of the Keeping, **Hamon Strand**, preached on stewardship.

In the autumn of **S.-1** the seam ran into a wall. The witnesses who reached the fen describe:

- **Fitted stone** across the whole seam, blocks so close a knife would not go between them, the
  ore running up to and under it.
- **Steps** beyond a first crack, worn in the middle as steps wear, but **the wrong height** for a
  human stride: too shallow, say some; too tall, say others.
- **Carving** worn almost smooth that nobody could afterwards draw alike. Lines like water, or only
  tool marks.
- **Air** through the crack, cold and then warm, steadily, as if something far down were breathing
  out. The lamps leaned toward the crack, not away.
- **No bones, no tools, no refuse.**

These are the **Deep-Seam Vaults**. Who made them, the book does not say (section 6 keeps the
guesses).

### The objections

The Slagcrown's **Hewers' Lodge** voted to refuse the ground. Its spokesman, **Ancel Brode**, a hewer
of thirty years, put it in the charter's terms: unknown ground, wrong air, the right of refusal.
Older hewers said what the Lodge would not write: their grandparents had said the deepest levels
"belonged to someone"; there were galleries at Hob's Height and Kell Tor walled up and never
reopened; the miners' word *the Tenant* was older than any of them. Two Bell Road parish priests
wrote to Abbot Strand asking that the seam not be blessed. Whether he answered is in the records
that went south.

### Why Vane ordered it

At midwinter S.-1 three things reached the Slagcrown in one month: **Blount's final schedule**, with
the word *stewardship* in it for the first time; **the Keeping's treasury** declining to extend the
loan unless the Black Seam was "pursued with diligence"; and **Abbot Strand's blessing** of the
ground beyond the wall, read in the chapel: to leave the seam was to refuse a gift.

Vane overruled the Lodge lawfully, taking the ground on his own hazard. He doubled the deep crews'
wages. Brode refused and was dismissed. Ebba Cray stayed because her crew stayed, and because, she
is supposed to have said, *somebody who knows the ground ought to be there.* Survivors remember Vane
in the lamp-room the night before, saying the Slagcrown was built by people who went into the dark
for their children and he would not be the Vane who stopped. Some remember it as courage. Some
remember that he did not go down himself.

### The Breaking

In the early spring of **S.0** the crews **fire-set** the wall, heating the stone with fires and
cracking it with water, the oldest way of breaking rock. It took eleven days. On the twelfth a
section fell inward. What witnesses saw, mostly second-hand:

- **A long warm draught** out of the breach that put out lamps forty paces back. It smelled of
  nothing, or hot stone, or mushrooms.
- **Grey dust** on the draught, settling over the Eleventh Gallery. Within a week the deep crews
  were coughing grey: the first record of the spores.
- **A sound**, low and long: water, wind in a shaft, or, said the lamp-boy **Kit Ewell**, who lived
  to be old in the fen, "a door closing a long way off".
- **Heat** rising in the lowest levels for weeks, until crews worked stripped to the waist and the
  pumps could not keep the water down.
- **The survey.** Six under Ebba Cray went through on the fourteenth day with lamps and line. Four
  came back, describing stairs going down, galleries wider than any the Slagcrown had cut, and more
  fitted stone. They would not describe anything else, then or later. Ebba Cray did not come back.

### The bells

The Slagcrown chapel bell was the alarm: stop work, open the stores, bring the crews up. In the
third week, when the deep crews came up coughing and the steward ordered work to go on, the
under-priest **Father Hamelin Lynde** rang it **against orders** until the steward's men pulled him off
the rope. Half the Citadel came up because of it.

The Bell Road chapels were built to pass the alarm on. Some did. Some priests, told by superiors
that the Slagcrown's bell was a disorder and not an alarm, **did not**. At **Hob's Height** in S.1,
when goblins first came up its shafts, the steward **Ralf Ottery** had the bell-ropes **cut** so
panic would not stop the ore carts due to leave. This is the archive's line: *some priests rang
against orders; elsewhere ropes were cut.*

## 3. S.0 to S.3, year by year

### S.0: the year of the Smothering

- **Early spring.** The Breaking. Within two months the Eleventh Gallery and the two levels above
  it are given up to heat, water and dust.
- **Midsummer.** The first **goblins** in the lower workings: small, grey, fast things that took lamp
  oil, tallow and food and could not be caught. Animals, the hewers said, and nothing since has
  shown otherwise. Their numbers grew every week.
- **Late summer: the first ashfall.** The moor north and east of the Slagcrown began to **smoke**;
  shepherds found the turf warm and springs running hot. In the harvest month a grey cloud stood
  over the highlands for nine days, then the wind turned and brought it down on the uplands and the
  fen as **ash**. The fen calls it **the Grey Harvest**: rye cut black and threshed grey, half never
  brought in. Whether the Breaking caused the venting, released it or only came first, nobody
  knows. The fen believes it caused it.
- **The first winter.** Early and hard. The **goblins came out** of the Citadels' lower works, the
  drains and the culverts under the Bell Road, down into the uplands and the fen edge, in
  **swarms** the first winter called hordes. They took food, metal, small animals, and people who
  were alone. Not an army: what comes out of a nest that has been broken open.

### S.1: the Year Without Harvest

Frost at midsummer; the sun a white disc through the ash on perhaps thirty days; the sown fields
rotted or froze. The fen dates its hunger from **the Year Without Harvest**.

- **Requisitions.** The Crown, through the lords, took carts, animals, grain and able men "for the
  relief of the Citadels". Harl's cart and son went north from Merewick that year
  (`keeping-and-merewick.md`) and neither came back. Villages received printed royal **receipts**.
  Many families still have them.
- **The ork packs.** In spring **orks** came up through the Slagcrown's shafts: heavy, grey, silent
  beasts fighting in packs behind shields and cleavers stripped from the Citadel's own armouries and
  dead. The first reports called them war-clans. They did not come to conquer. Every account agrees
  they came **fast, from below**, the way animals come out of a burning wood, and tore through
  whatever stood between them and the open air. The Citadels stood over the only ways up.
- **The Slagcrown abandoned (by midsummer).** The house lost its galleries level by level; survivors
  fled down the Bell Road and to Greystair. **Cerrin Vane's fate** is disputed. His last known letter,
  to Wexhaven, asks for soldiers. Some say he died at the inner gate, some that he went down into the
  levels; the southern copies, say those who have seen them, list him among those who reached
  Wexhaven. None of it can be proved.
- **Hob's Height walks out (autumn).** After goblins in its shafts and orks on its crag, Adam Haddon
  led his people down the Bell Road on foot, leaving the foundry cold. The **listeners** of the hills,
  who keep silence and wrap bells, tell their oldest stories about Hob's Height.
- **Refuge gates.** Greystair, Dunmarrow and walled towns further down, like Dunmere, opened their
  gates, then set **conditions**: residence, useful trade, provisions brought, current registration.
  Families holding covenant promises from the Lodges were turned away by the Citadels that had made
  them. The capacity limits were real. So was the favouritism.

### Kell Tor: the shut gate

What is known: in the autumn of **S.1** Kell Tor **mortared its gates from inside**. Riders from
Greystair found dressed stone across the gateway, the mortar still soft, the banner taken in. The
crag beacon, which had answered the Bell Road's signals for a century, burned three more nights and
**never again**. No message has come out since. No one in the book has been inside. Kell Tor had the
best pumps and granaries in the north, and kept the records of the old walled galleries in its
deepest levels. Ysmay Marne had written to the other Slag-Lords after the Breaking, a letter
remembered at Greystair only as "short and very angry".

What people say:

- **They are alive**, rationing years of stores behind the mortar. A shepherd named **Wilmot** saw
  **smoke** from the crag in S.17. Nobody else did.
- **They sealed something in.** Kell Tor knew what lay past its walled galleries and shut the gate
  to keep it there.
- **They went down**, and the mortar was the last thing they did on the surface.
- **They flooded it.** Marne stopped the pumps on purpose and let the tarn into the deep, and sealed
  the gate so no one would start them again.
- **Nothing remarkable.** They shut out the orks, the cough came in anyway, and they died with no one
  to write it down.

The beasts do not go near Kell Tor. That has a plain explanation too: there is nothing to eat there.

### S.2: the year of the gates

- **Greystair falls (spring).** It took in more than it could feed. By spring its terraces were full
  of the hungry and coughing, the refuge register had become a list of who ate, and the gates were
  guarded from inside as much as out. A crowd at the lower gate was refused; people with relatives
  in it forced the gate from within; in the confusion an ork pack came down the upper stairs. Rohese
  Tarrant was seen at the upper gate and is not recorded after.
- **Corpse-caps.** The first are noted in the common pits of S.1 and S.2; the first lime-pits are
  dated to S.2 in the parishes near the Split Kiln.
- **The Grain March (summer).** Dunmarrow's garrison was **ordered south** to escort the requisitioned
  grain of the north to Wexhaven "for distribution". It went down the Old Causeway past the villages
  that had grown it, holding their receipts. Corbet obeyed. Stripped of its garrison, Dunmarrow was
  overrun that autumn. Fen people still spit when they say *the Grain March*; Dunmarrow's survivors
  say they were promised the grain would come back up the road as bread.
- **The Retreat.** Through S.1 and S.2 the lords withdrew south: children first, then silver, papers
  and money, then the last wagons. Stewards were left with instructions and servants told to keep the
  properties "until arrangements could be made" (`halwen-house.md`, `the-last-gardens.md`). Priests
  left the same way, calling it a journey for relief. **The Retreat** is the fen's word for the whole
  withdrawal of gentry and clergy; the Flight South is its end.

### S.3: the Flight South and the burning

In the spring of **S.3**, when the inlet was clear of ice, **Aldric IV**, the court, the great
officers (Blount among them), the treasuries, archives and relics, the children of the great houses,
the Keeping's southern hierarchy and the Grain March's grain **sailed south** with the royal fleet.
The proclamation read in the harbour square promised the Crown went "to bring relief from our
friends beyond the sea, and to return with it before the second harvest". The city, the north, the
Watch, and the people who had walked to Wexhaven because they heard there would be ships, stayed.

The night after, **the harbour burned**: quays, warehouses, yards, and **every ship left in the
inlet**, down to the fishing boats, with much of the lower city. Whether it was ordered:

- **Ordered, to stop pursuit.** The rear guard under the harbour-marshal **Hugh Ardern** fired the
  ships so no one could follow the fleet. The fen's version.
- **Ordered, to stop a slaughter.** The crowd was seizing and overloading ships and drowning; Ardern
  chose fire over a massacre at the boats. A few who were there say this.
- **Not ordered.** The people left behind burned it, in rage or by accident in the riot. The version
  of priests who later returned claiming southern appointment.
- **Ordered, and further.** The rear guard was told to fire the granaries too. They were already
  empty.

Known: Ardern did not sail and is not recorded after S.3. Nobody knows where the fleet went. The
salt-pans east of Wexhaven did not burn, and salt has come up the Salt Trace in small amounts ever
since. Who boils it, the book does not say.

## 4. The Long Dark and after

### The Long Dark (S.3 to S.10)

- **Famine.** S.4, **the Hollow Winter**, was the worst. The stores opened in S.1 were gone, the
  receipts bought nothing, the seed grain was eaten; then the animals, their fodder, bark, reed root
  and leather. The archive's settlements began here, in households that opened stores together,
  some with careful accounts and some at knifepoint.
- **The beasts turning.** As the bestiary says: grazing failed, flocks were abandoned and went wild,
  and the fleeing lords left their kennels. Turned-out pigs became the **peat-boar** of the
  reedbeds. Kennel hounds and starving farm dogs ran together and greyed in the ash; the fen called
  them **Greymaw** by about S.7. Herons learned the common pits. Within ten years the farm animals of
  the fen had become its predators and pests.
- **The beasts from below spread.** Goblin swarms worked down into every drain and outbuilding. The
  ork packs never went back down; they took stone buildings with one way in, walls across roads and
  choke points, and held them as wolves hold a valley. The large things came slowest: **ogres** on the
  fen edge from S.4, the **quarry-troll**'s kind in the upland quarries from about S.6.
- **The burials and the lime.** Burial overwhelmed fuel, ground, labour and knowledge. Bodies lay in
  common pits, drains and sheepfolds; the corpse-caps rose in them, and those who cleared the pits
  began to cough. By S.6 **lime the dead or meet them** was law in most surviving parishes, and the
  Split Kiln's lime was worth as much for graves as for chimneys. Fire worked and cost fuel nobody
  had. Common graves were necessity in some places and sacrilege in others.
- **Ash-lung.** The grey cough had been in the Citadels since S.0. By about S.8 the fen healers had
  named its stages, and **"Ashbound"** had become the Watchhouse word for anyone who had stopped
  answering: a verdict, not a species.
- **The end (S.10).** The thaw lasted long enough for rye to ripen on the high fields at Merewick and
  the Last Gardens. A poor harvest. People remember it as the first.

### The Causeway Watch

The Watch's last **pay chest** came up the Salt Trace in the autumn of S.2. Its last **orders** came
in the spring of S.3, under the harbour-marshal's seal: "hold the Causeway open for the King's
return". Captain **Wenlock Darby** read them in the tower yard. They were the last royal orders
anyone in the Blackfen received.

The Watch kept the road. A third deserted in the first year, and the Long Dark killed more than
the beasts did, but those who stayed cleared the drains, burned the dead, rang the fog-bells and
held the tower. Unpaid, they took **toll in salt and food** from the carts they protected; the fen
called it robbery and paid, because the road was open. They kept the **muster book**, every name
that served and when each died or left, one of the few unbroken records in the fen.

- **S.12.** Too few to hold everything, the Watch withdrew from the Wayfarer's Watchhouse to the
  tower and the toll house. Refugees moved into the waystation within the month; it has been the
  survivors' refuge since, and its **warning book** (`bestiary.md`) was begun there.
- **S.20.** Serjeant **Agnes Kemble** took the tower after Darby's death.
- **S.26.** The last full muster: fourteen names.

For twenty-six years, **S.3 to S.29**, people the Crown had stopped paying kept the Old Causeway
open for travellers who had nothing to do with the Crown, against a return that never came.

### The Holding Years (S.10 to S.29)

Not a recovery: the years when the people left behind stopped dying faster than they were born.
The settlements took their present shape (Brackenreach's Upper Row, Merewick's mill and register,
Rook's Stair's terraces, the Last Gardens, the Watchhouse). Salt became money, priced in
**measures**. Registers were cut, sorted and fought over; gravediggers, midwives and chapel
cleaners held offices nobody gave them. The faith fractured into penitents, the Last Hearth, the
Unnamed and the listeners (`keeping-and-merewick.md`). **Collectors** began to arrive with certified
southern copies of old assessments, and later **priests claiming southern appointment**. Neither
will say plainly where the south they speak for is. People began to say **the ash is thinning**.
Some springs run a few days longer. No one can prove it.

### The walking blooms

In a few of the sick the bloom roots **before the body has finished dying**, and the caps fruit in a
body that still walks: **walking blooms**, or husks. Carriers talked of them in the west reedbeds
from about **S.22**; the warning book first wrote one down in **S.24**, when a carrier party lost two
people to the cloud from a husk they had killed at arm's length. Some say husks are new; some say
they were always in the deep fen and nobody came back to tell. The reports have grown every year,
and the low ground now called the **Spore Hollow** has had them for at least five.

### The fall of the Causeway tower (autumn S.29)

An **ork pack** that had worked the fen's edge since S.27 came onto the Causeway under a
**cinder-squall**, when nothing could be heard over the wind. Nine of the Watch were at the tower.
The muster book's last entry, in a hand nobody at the Watchhouse knows, gives four names as dead and
a date. The rest scattered. At least one reached the Watchhouse with the sword they were issued.
*You held the Causeway tower until you didn't.*

The pack walled the road at the **choke point** with rubble from the tower, leaving a narrow gap,
and piles behind the wall what it takes from the dead. For the first time in a hundred and
seventy-seven years the Old Causeway is shut at the fen end. That is where the winter of S.30 finds
the Watchhouse.

## 6. Contested histories

Each version settles who owes whom. The record supports none completely.

**The southern hierarchy** (returning priests, collectors' papers): the Crown went south lawfully to
organise relief and keeps its claims; the Keeping went to preserve the covenant; the Breaking was a
calamity everyone shares, hewers and lord alike; rioters burned the harbour; obedience comes before
accounts, and the old assessments stand. *The record:* the Keeping blessed the descent, held the
Slagcrown's debt and pressed for "diligence"; its senior clergy took the first ships; the Grain
March receipts were royal and the grain left on the fleet. Some who sailed truly meant to bring aid.

**The local faithful:** a lord was their lord only while he kept them. Vane broke the covenant
first, Aldric IV worst; what the departed left belongs to whoever stayed and worked it. Some say the
Smothering is the dark the covenant held back, let in by broken promises. *The record:* the
covenant broke in every direction. Greystair turned away families holding the Lodges' own promises,
and many who stayed hid stores from their neighbours.

**The Citadel survivors:** the hewers warned and were overruled; the Breaking was murder done with a
ledger, and they name Vane, Strand and Blount. Some Slagcrown families say the opposite: Vane was
forced, the Crown would have turned them out anyway, and the Tenant was coming up whether anyone dug
or not. *The record:* the order was lawful under the hazard clause; the Lodge did refuse; whether the
venting would have come without the Breaking cannot be known.

**The Causeway Watch:** it kept its oath when the Crown did not, and its toll was wages. *The
record:* it held the road twenty-six years, and also took from carriers who could not refuse, shut
the tower to travellers in bad winters, and lost a third of its strength to desertion in a year. The
muster book names the deserters as faithfully as the dead.

**The listeners**, when they speak: noise reaches unwanted listeners; the bells should never have
rung; the Hush is not to be named. They tell Hob's Height as people who stopped ringing and lived.
*The record:* Hob's Height's ropes were cut for the ore carts, and its people walked out.

**Who built the Vaults.** The guesses the fen keeps, none chosen:

- An older people of Orlen, before the Crown, who mined the deep and sealed it for a reason they
  did not write down.
- The miners' Tenant, and the Vaults are its house.
- Nobody: the fitted stone is natural cleavage, the steps slumped bedding, seen by frightened
  people in lamp-light. (The four who came back from the survey would not say so.)
- The Citadels themselves in the charter's first century, and the walled galleries at Hob's Height
  and Kell Tor are the same forgotten work.

**What the beasts show.** Goblins and orks are not witnesses; they are animals, and what they give
is behaviour. **Goblins** came up first and fastest and stayed near the surface, in drains and
outbuildings, as if the nearest dark were enough. They scavenge, stab with crude shivs, throw
stones, swarm, and run from anything that fights back. **Orks** came up harder and **never went back
down**. They fight in packs behind stolen shields, drilled by instinct and dominance like wolves in
armour, and they hold walls and choke points. They make **no noise**, and the Dead Calm, when
everything is heard, drives them to ground. Every guess about what they fled (that it hunts by
sound; that it is the earth's heat itself; that it is still coming up) starts from those two facts.
Neither proves anything. Some fen folk say the beasts were sent up as punishment for the Breaking.
The book records the saying and nothing that supports it.

## 7. Survivor voices

Plain ASCII, ready to place as notes. Each is someone remembering; none is necessarily right.

1. **A receipt kept in a tin.** "Received of the parish of Merewick, for the relief of the
   Citadels, rye, eleven measures. To be repaid in kind. By order of the King." Under it, in
   charcoal: "Still waiting. S.19."
2. **Kit Ewell, Slagcrown lamp-boy, in old age.** "People ask what it sounded like. Like a door
   closing a long way off. That is all I will say. I was eleven. Lamps lean away from wind. Mine
   leaned toward the hole."
3. **A woman at the Greystair gate.** "They asked for my registration. I had the Lodge's paper with
   my husband's mark and the steward's seal. They said that paper was for the Slagcrown and this was
   Greystair. My husband died in the Slagcrown. The paper was why I came."
4. **A fen carter, on the Grain March.** "We watched our rye go by on our own carts with Dunmarrow
   soldiers on top. My father held up his receipt like it would stop them. The serjeant nodded at
   him. Nodded. As if they were square."
5. **Scratched on a Bell Road chapel wall.** "Bell rang from the north three days. Father said it was
   a disorder. Father went south. We stayed. We ring it now."
6. **The Watch muster book.** "S.26. Muster at the tower. Fourteen present. Pay: none. Orders: as
   before. Road: open." Underneath, another hand: "Hold it open for the King's return. We are not
   holding it for him."
7. **A woman who walked to Wexhaven.** "We saw the sails go out in the morning and the fire start at
   night. I don't know who lit it. I know nobody on the quay was surprised."
8. **An old hewer, on Cerrin Vane.** "He wasn't a bad lord. That's what nobody wants to hear. He paid
   the widows. He knew our names. He just could not be the one who stopped. I think he thought the
   dark owed him."
9. **Left at the foot of Kell Tor.** "Knocked at the gate, S.17. Stone and mortar all the way across.
   Put my ear to it a long while. Nothing. Left bread on the step. In the morning it was gone. Could
   have been rats. Could have been anything."
10. **The Watch Deserter, at the fire.** "We held it twenty-six years for a man who left on a boat.
    Then the wind came up so you couldn't hear yourself, and they were on the wall before anyone
    shouted. Don't ask me who's to blame. I was there. That's all I know."
